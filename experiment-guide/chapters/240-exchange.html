Two single qubits, however good, do not make a computer. They need an interaction that can be switched on hard for a two-qubit gate and switched off almost completely the rest of the time. Spin qubits get it from the same tunnelling that the previous chapters measured and tuned: when two electrons can hop between their dots, their energy depends on their relative spin orientation. This chapter explains where that exchange interaction comes from, how it looks in a device with a micromagnet, how to measure it over three orders of magnitude, and how to control it with the barrier gate at a point where it is protected from charge noise.

Where exchange comes from

Put one electron in each dot, in (1,1). If the tunnel coupling tc is not zero, each electron can make a brief virtual excursion into the other dot, into (0,2) or (2,0), and come back. That excursion is possible only if the two spins form a singlet, because two electrons in the same orbital must have opposite spins (Chapter 16). The singlet therefore gains a little energy from these virtual hops, by second-order perturbation, while the triplets do not. The energy difference between triplet and singlet is the exchange energy J, and the two spins behave as if coupled by

\[ H_J = J\left(\mathbf{S}_1\cdot\mathbf{S}_2 - \tfrac{1}{4}\right), \qquad J \approx \frac{4t_c^2}{U} \text{ at the symmetric point,} \]

where U is the energy cost of the doubly occupied state, the charging energy reduced by the interdot charging energy, about 5.6 meV in our device. This simple Hubbard estimate captures the essential behaviour, the quadratic dependence on tc, but real dots deviate from it, especially at large J, where the orbitals deform .

Worked numbers: how small tc must be

At the readout setting, tc = 20 µeV and J = 4 × (20 µeV)²/5.6 meV ≈ 0.29 µeV ≈ 69 MHz. Far too strong to leave on.

For J = 5 MHz, a convenient value for a two-qubit gate, tc must be about 5.4 µeV. For J below 30 kHz, "off", tc must be below 0.42 µeV.

With tc changing by a factor e for every 10 mV of vB2 (Chapter 12), J changes by e for every 5 mV. Going from 5 MHz to 23 kHz, a factor of 220, takes 27 mV of barrier: vB2 = −13 mV for the gate, −40 mV for off.

Exchange in a magnetic-field gradient

In our device the two qubits differ in frequency by ΔEZ/h = 300 MHz, much more than the J of a few megahertz we will use. Exchange tries to mix the antiparallel states ↑↓ and ↓↑ into singlet and triplet, but a large Zeeman difference keeps them apart. The flip-flop part of the interaction is suppressed by the ratio J/ΔEZ, and what remains is an Ising-type coupling: the energy of each qubit depends on the state of the other . In the spectrum, each qubit's resonance splits into two lines, separated by J, one for each state of the partner. RIKEN's analysis gives the four transition frequencies as EZ ± (ΔẼZ ± J)/2 with ΔẼZ = √(ΔEZ² + J²) .

This is good news. The conditional energy shift is exactly what a controlled-phase gate needs (Chapter 25), and it can be read directly from the qubits' spectrum (Figure 24.1).

Measuring J from 10 kHz to 20 MHz

No single method covers the whole range, because J changes by three orders of magnitude. Two complement each other.

  • Conditional spectroscopy (J ≳ 1 MHz). Prepare qubit 2 in ↓ or ↑, pulse the barrier, and find qubit 1's resonance with a weak drive during the pulse. The difference between the two frequencies is J. Princeton measured J this way, with a long, low-power probe on the left qubit, and found that 20 mV on their barrier changed J by about 10 MHz .
  • Decoupled controlled-phase oscillations (J from about 10 kHz to 1 MHz). Put qubit 1 on the equator, pulse the barrier for half the time, apply π pulses to both qubits, pulse the barrier again, and project with a final π/2 pulse. The π pulses cancel all single-qubit phases, from the gradient shifts and from slow noise, but not the conditional phase, which oscillates at J/2 as a function of the total exchange time (Figure 24.3). QuTech used this sequence on their six-qubit device .

A practical recipe, close to QuTech's: measure J where it is large and easy, in the upper part of the barrier range; fit an exponential; extrapolate to set the pulse amplitudes for any J you need; then check a few low values with decoupled oscillations . The fit, J(vB2), is a calibrated function, not a number, and it drifts: it is re-measured as part of the daily calibration.

Barrier control and the symmetry point

There are two ways to make J larger. One is to tilt the double dot towards (0,2) or (2,0) with the detuning, which lowers the energy cost of the virtual hop. This is how exchange was first controlled, and how singlet–triplet qubits are often operated (Chapter 27). The other is to raise tc with the barrier, keeping the dots level at ε = 0. At that point J depends on ε only quadratically (Figure 24.2, right): small charge-noise fluctuations of the detuning do not change J to first order. This symmetric operation, introduced for singlet–triplet qubits in GaAs and adopted by HRL and others, gave a large improvement in the quality of exchange oscillations . Princeton's CNOT and QuTech's high-fidelity two-qubit gates operate there .

The symmetry point has to be found, not assumed. QuTech did it by sweeping the detuning during decoupled exchange pulses inside a Ramsey sequence: the resulting pattern is symmetric about the symmetry point . Two details matter. The barrier must be pulsed as a virtual gate, with compensating pulses on both plungers, or the barrier pulse itself would detune the dots (Chapter 11). And the pulses need the pre-distortion of Chapter 13: because J depends exponentially on the barrier, a 1% overshoot of a 27 mV barrier pulse changes J by about 5%.

Side effects of a barrier pulse

  • Single-qubit phases. Opening the barrier moves both electrons slightly, along the micromagnet's gradient, and their frequencies shift by about 0.12 MHz/mV (Figure 24.1). During a gate pulse, each qubit therefore picks up a phase of its own, unrelated to the other's state. It is large, but it is reproducible, and is cancelled by virtual Z corrections or by echo pulses (Chapter 25). QuTech found these shifts linear in the barrier voltage .
  • Charge noise on J. Because J depends exponentially on the barrier, voltage noise at the barrier is noise on J: dJ/dV = J/(5 mV). Slow charge noise equivalent to 0.4 mV, which QuTech estimated from conditional dephasing, changes a 5 MHz J by 0.4 MHz . That is why gates use the smallest J that is still fast enough, and why the quality of exchange oscillations, the number of periods before they decay, is a figure of merit for a two-qubit gate.
  • The residual coupling. At "off", J = 23 kHz, not zero. Over a 1 µs idle, that accumulates a conditional phase of 2π × 23 kHz × 1 µs ≈ 8°. For good idles and single-qubit gates, the on/off ratio must be large, at least a hundred. QuTech reached Joff ≲ 30–50 kHz only by pulsing neighbouring barriers negative as well .
When it goes wrong

What you see: J cannot be turned off: even at the most negative barrier you can safely apply, the conditional splitting stays above 200 kHz.

Likely causes: the dots are too close or too large; the barrier gate has a weak lever arm on the tunnel coupling; or the dots are not at the symmetry point but tilted, where J is larger.

What to do: check the detuning first, by finding the symmetry point at a moderate J. Use neighbouring gates in the virtual barrier: plungers pulled slightly negative make the dots smaller and the barrier wider. If none of this works, the residual coupling must be included in the circuit design, for example by echoing it out during idles.

Check yourself

1. Why does exchange favour the singlet, and not the triplet?

Show answer
Exchange comes from virtual hops into the doubly occupied dot. Two electrons in the same orbital must be in a spin singlet (Pauli exclusion), so only the singlet component can make the hop and gain the second-order energy shift. The triplets cannot, so they stay at their unshifted energy, and the singlet ends up lower by J.

2. The tunnel coupling at the CZ setting doubles because of a gate drift. What happens to J and to a CZ gate calibrated before the drift?

Show answer
J ∝ tc², so J quadruples, from 5 to 20 MHz. A pulse calibrated to give ∫J dt = ½ now gives 2, a conditional phase of 4π instead of π: completely wrong. This is why J(V) is recalibrated regularly.

3. Why is the flip-flop part of exchange suppressed in our device, and why is that useful?

Show answer
The flip-flop term swaps ↑↓ and ↓↑, but these differ in energy by ΔEZ = 300 MHz, so a coupling of J ≈ 5 MHz cannot drive the transition efficiently. Only the diagonal, Ising-like part remains, which shifts each qubit's energy depending on the other: a controlled phase, which is a natural two-qubit gate and does not move population between the qubits.
