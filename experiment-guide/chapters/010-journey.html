When a group says "we have a working spin qubit", it means they have a list of numbers: how well they can prepare the spin, how fast and how accurately they can rotate it, how long it remembers its state, and how reliably they can read it out. Every number on that list was measured, and every measurement needed numbers measured before it. This chapter lays out that chain once, from beginning to end, so the rest of the book never feels like a list of disconnected procedures.

Start from the end

Imagine the last experiment in a long campaign: randomized benchmarking of a single spin qubit. You prepare the electron's spin pointing down, apply a random sequence of a few hundred rotations that should, ideally, bring it back to where it started, then measure it. You repeat this for many random sequences and many lengths, and the rate at which the result decays tells you the average error of one gate. The output is a single number, perhaps 99.85%.

Now ask what you needed in order to run that experiment. Three things, at least:

  1. You had to prepare a known spin state. That means you already knew how to load one electron into the dot with a known spin, either by letting it relax in a magnetic field or by an energy-selective trick that only lets one spin direction in.
  2. You had to rotate the spin by exactly the right angles. That means you knew the spin's resonance frequency to within a few kilohertz, the microwave amplitude that gives a 90° rotation, and the phase that defines the rotation axis.
  3. You had to measure the spin in one shot. That means you had a way to turn "up" or "down" into a charge signal, and a charge sensor fast and quiet enough to see a single electron move in microseconds.

Each of those, in turn, rests on something earlier. You cannot find the resonance frequency without a spin readout to tell you when you have hit it. You cannot read out a spin before you have a single electron trapped in a dot, with tunnelling rates to its neighbours set to the right values. You cannot trap a single electron without a charge sensor to count electrons. You cannot form a sensor without first making the device conduct at all. And none of it happens until the device is cold, wired and quiet. Followed backwards, the chain reaches all the way to the moment the chip is mounted on its circuit board.

Every measurement in a spin-qubit experiment exists to produce a number that a later measurement needs.

This is the single most useful idea for making sense of what an experimental group does all day. When you see someone spend a week measuring tunnelling rates, it is not because tunnelling rates are interesting in themselves (sometimes they are), but because the spin readout cannot be set up without them.

Notice the change halfway through. Stages 1 to 4 are all about charge: making electrons appear, counting them, choosing exactly how many sit in each dot and how easily they hop. The spin, which is the actual qubit, does not appear until stage 5. This surprises newcomers, but it is how every spin-qubit experiment works. A spin's magnetic moment is far too small to detect directly. All spin measurements are done by converting spin into charge, and that conversion only works once the charge configuration is under complete control. More than half the effort on a new device is spent before anyone measures a spin.

Why the order is fixed

Some steps can be swapped or repeated, but most of the order is forced by dependencies. Figure 1.2 shows the main ones. Read it as "you need this to do that".

Three dependencies are worth spelling out, because they explain choices that otherwise look arbitrary:

  • The lever arm turns voltages into energies. Gate voltages are what you set, but physics happens in energy. A lever arm α of 0.1 eV/V means 1 mV on the plunger moves the dot level by 100 µeV. Without it you cannot know whether a 0.5 mV step moves the level by more or less than the Zeeman energy, and spin readout depends on exactly that comparison. So the lever arm is measured early (Chapter 8), and almost every later chapter uses it.
  • Electron temperature limits readout. Spin readout distinguishes two energies separated by the Zeeman energy. If the electrons in the reservoir are thermally smeared over a comparable energy, the wrong spin occasionally tunnels and the readout makes mistakes. The ratio of Zeeman energy to thermal energy is therefore one of the first things a readout depends on (Chapter 15).
  • Readout limits everything after it. Once the readout works, every later experiment is "do something, then read out". If the readout misidentifies the state 10% of the time, every Rabi oscillation, every Ramsey fringe and every benchmarking curve has its contrast reduced, and you need more repetitions to see the same thing. Groups therefore invest heavily in readout before doing anything ambitious with the qubit.

Energy scales decide what is possible

Almost every design decision and every tuning step in this field comes down to comparing energies. A qubit works only if certain energies are well separated from each other. Figure 1.3 places the important ones for our device on one logarithmic axis.

Read the ladder from the bottom up, and each rung has a job:

  • The charging energy (millielectronvolts) is the cost of adding one more electron to a dot. Because it is so large, the number of electrons on a dot is a sharp integer that stays put unless you deliberately change it.
  • The orbital spacing (about 3 meV) keeps the electron in its lowest orbital, so the dot behaves like a simple two-level spin system and not a little atom with many states.
  • The valley splitting (100 to 300 µeV in good silicon devices) is specific to silicon. Silicon's conduction band has two nearly degenerate minima, or valleys, along the growth direction. The interface lifts this degeneracy, but only by a small and device-dependent amount. If the valley splitting is not comfortably larger than the Zeeman energy and the thermal energy, a second set of states joins the party and spoils both readout and coherence (Chapter 14).
  • The Zeeman energy (64 µeV at 0.55 T) is the qubit itself: the energy between spin down and spin up. It must be large compared with the thermal energy so that the spin can be initialized and read out, and small compared with the valley and orbital energies so that the qubit stays a clean two-level system.
  • The thermal energy kBT at an electron temperature of 50 mK is about 4.3 µeV. Every readout scheme needs it to be well below the energies it relies on.
  • The control energies, the Rabi frequency and the exchange coupling, are a million times smaller than the charging energy. Small control energies mean gentle, precise rotations. The price is that the rotations take tens to hundreds of nanoseconds, which must be short compared with the coherence times.

Keep this ladder in mind. Whenever a tuning step looks mysterious, the reason is almost always one of these comparisons.

Time scales decide how fast you can work

The second ladder is in time (Figure 1.4). It tells you two things: which operations fit inside which coherence time, and how long an experiment takes to acquire.

The bottom of the ladder is physics: a gate takes about 100 ns, the spin dephases in microseconds when left alone, and an echo extends that to tens or hundreds of microseconds. The top of the ladder is life in the lab: the device drifts over minutes to hours, so calibrations must be repeated, and a cooldown takes days, so you do not warm up casually.

The middle of the ladder, the readout, decides how fast you learn. Each data point in a qubit experiment is a probability, estimated by repeating the same sequence many times. How long one repetition takes is set mostly by the readout and by how long you wait for the spin to reset.

Worked numbers: how long does a Rabi chevron take?

A standard early experiment, the Rabi chevron (Chapter 19), scans the microwave frequency over 100 values and the burst duration over 100 values. That is 10,000 points, each repeated 200 times to estimate a probability: 2 million shots.

With Elzerman readout (Chapter 15) a shot takes about 1 ms: a few hundred microseconds to load and initialize, the pulse itself, and a readout window of a few hundred microseconds. Two million shots × 1 ms = 2000 s, about 33 minutes.

With Pauli-blockade readout and fast reflectometry (Chapter 16), a shot can take 20 µs. Two million shots × 20 µs = 40 s.

The same measurement is 50 times faster. Over a week of calibrations, that difference decides whether you can afford to recalibrate every hour. This is why so much effort goes into faster readout.

What a single data point really is

A qubit measurement gives one bit: up or down. A probability such as "the spin-up probability after this pulse" is estimated by repeating the experiment N times and counting. The estimate has a statistical uncertainty set by the binomial distribution,

\[ \sigma_P = \sqrt{\frac{P(1-P)}{N}}, \]

which is largest at P = 0.5. With N = 200 shots, σ is about 3.5% at P = 0.5, and halving it needs four times as many shots. This is why experiments are planned around the shot count, and why a fast readout is worth so much: precision costs time, and the cost grows as the square of the precision you want.

The measured probability is also not the true one. If the readout misidentifies spin down as spin up with probability α and spin up as spin down with probability β, the probability you record is

\[ P_{\text{meas}} = \alpha + (1 - \alpha - \beta)\,P_{\text{true}}. \]

The factor 1 − α − β is the visibility. A Rabi oscillation that should swing from 0 to 1 instead swings from α to 1 − β. A visibility of 0.7, common in older experiments, means every oscillation is squeezed into 70% of its full range. Chapter 17 shows how to measure α and β honestly, and Chapter 23 how benchmarking protocols are designed to be insensitive to them.

Check yourself

1. A colleague proposes searching for the spin resonance before the charge sensor is tuned, "to save time". Why will this not work?

Show answer
Finding the resonance means applying microwaves and checking whether the spin flipped. The only way to see a spin flip is through spin-to-charge conversion, which needs a working charge sensor and a dot with one electron and known tunnel rates. Without the readout there is no signal, so the search cannot tell when it hits the resonance.

2. Using 1 µeV ↔ 11.6 mK, what electron temperature would make the thermal energy equal to one tenth of a 64 µeV Zeeman splitting? Is that easy to reach?

Show answer
One tenth of 64 µeV is 6.4 µeV, which corresponds to 6.4 × 11.6 ≈ 74 mK. Electron temperatures of 40 to 100 mK are common in well-filtered dilution refrigerators, so this is achievable but not automatic. It depends on filtering and thermalization (Chapter 4).

3. A Ramsey scan has 80 delay values and you want ±2.5% uncertainty at P = 0.5. How many shots per point, and how long does the scan take with 1 ms shots?

Show answer
σ = √(0.25/N) = 0.025 gives N = 0.25/0.000625 = 400 shots. 80 × 400 = 32,000 shots, which is 32 s at 1 ms per shot.
