This last chapter runs through the experiment once more, as it happens in time: from a bonded chip to benchmarked two-qubit gates, then into the routine of running a device day after day. It is a summary, but also a map. Each step lists what it needs from earlier steps and what it hands on, so that when something fails you know where to look.

Two things about this timeline are worth saying plainly. First, it is optimistic. Every step can take several times longer when a device misbehaves, and many devices never get past Day 2 or Day 7: a shorted gate, a barrier that does not pinch off, a valley splitting too small for readout. The first weeks are as much about deciding whether a device is worth continuing with as about tuning it. Second, the order is not negotiable. Each step uses numbers from the previous ones, and skipping one, for example going to spin readout before the tunnel rates are measured, usually costs more time than doing it.

A routine week

Once a device works, the rhythm changes from discovery to production. A typical week with our device might look like this:

  • Every morning: check the fridge, then run the calibration graph from the top. Sensor and readout first, then frequencies and amplitudes, then the J law and the CZ. Look at AllXY and a short RB for each qubit before starting the day's experiments.
  • During the day: experiments run with the frequency, readout and sensor checks interleaved every 10–30 minutes. Long runs are split into blocks with calibrations in between, and the order of measurements is randomized so that drift affects everything equally.
  • Overnight: long measurements such as GST, two-qubit RB or noise spectroscopy, with automatic recalibration and with every calibration result logged. Check in the morning that nothing jumped.
  • Weekly: full recalibration including virtual gates and crosstalk; T1, T2* and echo for each qubit, to see slow changes in the device; check the readout chain's gain and the tank's resonance frequency.
  • After a large charge jump: stop, find the new operating point with a quick charge stability map, re-run the calibration graph from the bottom, and note the event. If jumps become frequent, a short warm-up to a few kelvin, or a full thermal cycle, sometimes resets the traps, at the cost of re-tuning.

What the device taught us

Looking back at the passport, the device's whole performance follows from a handful of physical numbers: the lever arm and charging energy that set the voltage scales, the electron temperature and Zeeman energy that set readout, the valley splitting that sets relaxation and blockade, the micromagnet's gradients that set driving and dephasing, the 1/f charge noise that sets coherence and exchange quality, and the tunnel couplings that set everything involving two electrons. Each of those was measured by a simple experiment early on, and each reappeared, sometimes chapters later, as the limit of something more complex. That chain from simple measurements to qubit performance is what a spin-qubit experiment really is. Following it once, step by step, is the best preparation for following it on a new device, in a different material, or with a different encoding.
