The device in this book is one design among several, and four ideas from the others are worth knowing well, because they change the experiment in instructive ways. Holes in germanium replace the micromagnet with the material's own spin–orbit coupling. Hopping spins replace microwaves with baseband pulses. Flopping-mode qubits trade a little charge character for much stronger driving. And shuttling moves the electrons themselves, so that qubits no longer need to sit next to each other to interact.

Holes in germanium

In a strained germanium quantum well between SiGe barriers, the charge carriers can be holes rather than electrons. The plunger voltages become negative, the ohmic contacts are often aluminium, and the effective mass is light, so dots are larger and easier to define . Three properties change the qubit experiment substantially.

  • Strong spin–orbit coupling. A hole's spin is tied to its orbital motion, so an oscillating voltage on a gate drives spin resonance directly, with no micromagnet. Rabi frequencies above 100 MHz were reached early, with two-qubit CX gates in 75 ns , and the first hole qubit in planar germanium came in 2018 .
  • No valleys. The valence band has no valley degeneracy, so the valley-splitting issues of Chapter 14 disappear.
  • An anisotropic g-tensor. The g-factor depends strongly on the field direction, from about 0.1 in the plane to over 10 out of the plane, and on the gate voltages. That gives each qubit a different frequency and quantization axis, which is a resource for control but also a source of variability and of sensitivity to charge noise. Operating at a sweet spot of the g-tensor, QuTech measured T2* = 9.2 µs, limited by 1/f charge noise .

Germanium arrays have grown quickly: four qubits with universal control , a 16-dot crossbar with shared control lines , a two-dimensional ten-qubit array , simultaneous single-qubit driving at 99.992% native gate fidelity , and an 18-qubit modular array with average single-qubit fidelity of 99.8% and simultaneous operation of all qubits . Hole qubits also work in silicon, in CMOS-style transistors and nanowires .

Worked numbers: why holes run at low field

With an in-plane hole g-factor of 0.2, a 15 GHz qubit would need B = 15 GHz/(0.2 × 14 GHz/T) ≈ 5.4 T. Germanium experiments therefore run at tens to hundreds of millitesla and qubit frequencies of a few hundred megahertz to a few gigahertz.

At 1 GHz, EZ is only 4 µeV, about kBT at 50 mK, so Elzerman readout is impossible. Germanium experiments use Pauli spin blockade, often latched, for readout .

Hopping spins: rotation without microwaves

If neighbouring dots have different quantization axes, as germanium holes do because of their g-tensors, then moving a spin from one dot to the next suddenly changes the axis it precesses about. A spin that hops into a dot with a tilted axis and waits there for part of a precession period has been rotated, just as if it had been driven. Hopping back and forth with the right timing implements any single-qubit gate with baseband pulses only. QuTech demonstrated hopping-based single-qubit gates of 99.97%, shuttling of 99.992% per hop and a two-qubit gate of 99.3% in germanium . The idea also works for electrons in silicon, where a micromagnet's stray field provides the tilted axes. In a 2 × 2 silicon array, hopping gates of at least 99.50% matched the 99.54% of resonant EDSR gates, without the pulse-induced frequency shifts of Chapter 22 .

Hopping spins are a reminder that "single-spin qubit" and "microwave control" are separate choices. A single spin can be driven by baseband pulses too, if the device provides tilted axes, so the advantages of exchange-based encodings, which avoid microwaves, can partly be had without encoding a qubit in several spins.

Flopping mode: borrowing the charge

In ordinary EDSR the electron sits in one dot and is shaken by about half a nanometre (Chapter 18). If instead a single electron is shared between two dots at zero detuning, with tc comparable to the Zeeman energy, a small voltage moves it across the whole double dot, a hundred nanometres, through a large field gradient. The spin then couples much more strongly to the electric field. Princeton showed that this flopping-mode EDSR is almost three orders of magnitude more efficient than conventional driving . The price is that the qubit now has partly charge character and is more exposed to charge noise, but theory shows a sweet spot where that coupling is suppressed .

The same charge–spin hybridization makes a spin talk to a microwave photon in a superconducting resonator. Strong coupling between a single electron spin and a photon was demonstrated in silicon by two groups in 2018 , and two spins 4 mm apart were made to interact through a resonator . That is one of the routes towards long-range connections between spin-qubit modules.

Shuttling: moving the qubit

Exchange works only between neighbours, which makes it hard to connect qubits that are far apart, or to leave room between qubits for wiring and electronics. Moving the electron itself, with its spin intact, solves both problems. Two ways of doing it are in use (Figure 29.1).

  • Bucket brigade. QuTech moved a spin back and forth through a four-dot silicon array up to 250 times . RIKEN used shuttling to make a two-qubit gate: moving the electrons apart switched exchange off with an on/off ratio above 1000, and each shuttle preserved 99.6% of the spin coherence . In germanium, spin states were shuttled over effective distances beyond 300 µm, and superpositions over 9 µm, extended to 49 µm with dynamical decoupling .
  • Conveyor mode. Jülich and Aachen moved a single electron through a 420 nm Si/SiGe channel with four sinusoidal control signals, with a fidelity of 99.42% including a reversal of direction . The same technique maps the valley splitting along the channel with nanometre resolution, using spin pairs as probes . Valleys are one of the main limits of shuttling in silicon, because an electron moving over a spot of small valley splitting can be excited into the upper valley.

Shuttling changes the architecture: qubits can sit in sparse arrays connected by shuttling buses, rather than in dense blocks. QuTech used a shuttling bus with four "bus stops" to run weight-four parity checks, the basic operation of the surface code, and to entangle five qubits .

Check yourself

1. A germanium hole qubit has g = 0.3 in the plane. What field gives a 2 GHz qubit, and what is EZ/kBT at 50 mK?

Show answer
B = 2 GHz/(0.3 × 14 GHz/T) ≈ 0.48 T. EZ = 2 GHz × 4.14 µeV/GHz ≈ 8.3 µeV, about 1.9 kBT. Far too small for energy-selective readout, which is why blockade readout is used.

2. Why does a hopping gate need dots with different quantization axes?

Show answer
If both dots had the same axis, moving the spin between them would only change its precession rate about the same axis, a z rotation. A tilted axis in the second dot makes the spin precess about a different direction while it waits there, which rotates it away from the original axis: a real x or y rotation.

3. What limits conveyor-mode shuttling in Si/SiGe that is much less of an issue in germanium?

Show answer
The valley splitting varies along the channel. Where it becomes small, the moving electron can be excited into the upper valley, which changes its spin dynamics and can cause errors. Germanium holes have no valley degeneracy.
