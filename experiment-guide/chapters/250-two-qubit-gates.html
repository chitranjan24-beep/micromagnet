With exchange measured and under control, it can be shaped into gates. There are three natural ones for spins: a controlled phase made by pulsing J, a controlled rotation made by driving one of the split resonance lines, and a SWAP made by letting exchange exchange the spins. Which one a group uses depends on the ratio of J to the Zeeman energy difference, on the readout, and on taste. This chapter builds each of them for our device, with the calibrations they need and the errors that limit them.

The controlled-phase gate

In our device J ≪ ΔEZ, so exchange acts as an Ising coupling: while it is on, the antiparallel states gain a phase relative to the parallel ones. After a pulse with ∫J dt = ½ (J in hertz), the conditional phase is exactly π, and the pulse is a controlled-Z gate, CZ, up to single-qubit phases . In words: the state |11⟩ gets a minus sign, nothing else changes. Combined with single-qubit rotations, CZ gives a CNOT or any other two-qubit gate.

Two requirements shape the pulse. First, it must be adiabatic with respect to the Zeeman difference: J must change slowly compared to ΔEZ/h, or the antiparallel states will partly swap, an error that leaves the computational basis. With ΔEZ/h = 300 MHz, ramps of a few tens of nanoseconds suffice. Second, J should be smooth, so that the gate's spectrum is narrow and its errors predictable. Groups use a cosine window or a Tukey window, a flat top with cosine ramps . Because J depends exponentially on the barrier, the barrier pulse that produces a cosine in J is not itself a cosine: it is the logarithm of the desired J(t) (Figure 25.1). QuTech defined their pulses in energy and converted them to voltage through the measured J(V) .

Worked numbers: the CZ pulse

For a cosine window J(t) = Jmax[1 − cos(2πt/T)]/2, the integral is JmaxT/2. Setting it to ½ gives T = 1/Jmax: with Jmax = 5 MHz, T = 200 ns.

A 15 MHz peak would give a 67 ns gate, but with three times the sensitivity to charge noise on J, and more heating from larger barrier pulses.

Calibrating the CZ

Three things must be right after the pulse, and each has its own measurement.

  1. The conditional phase. A Ramsey measurement on qubit 1 with qubit 2 in |0⟩ and then in |1⟩ measures the phase difference the pulse produces. Adjust the pulse amplitude (or duration) until it is π. QuTech fine-tuned the CZ duration with exactly such a decoupled Ramsey measurement .
  2. The single-qubit phases. The barrier pulse moves both electrons in the gradient (Chapter 24), and each picks up a phase independent of the other. Measure each with a Ramsey sequence and cancel it with a virtual Z gate after the CZ. In arrays the pulse also shifts the phases of spectator qubits; QuTech calibrated thirty such "CZ phase crosstalk" terms for six qubits .
  3. Population leakage. If the ramps are too fast, some population of ↑↓ and ↓↑ swaps. Measure the probability of finding the partner flipped after a CZ, and lengthen the ramps if it is significant.

Alternatively, the single-qubit phases can be cancelled physically by splitting the CZ into two halves with π pulses on both qubits in between, a "decoupled CZ". This also echoes out slow single-qubit noise during the gate, at the cost of two extra pulses. Delft's first programmable two-qubit processor used decoupled CZ gates .

What limits the CZ

A conditional phase error δφ gives a gate infidelity of about 0.15 δφ² for a two-qubit gate. The main source of δφ is noise on J. Slow charge noise on the barrier, equivalent to a few tenths of a millivolt, changes J by several percent, so a gate calibrated to π overshoots or undershoots by a few percent of π from shot to shot .

Worked numbers: our CZ error budget

Slow noise equivalent to 0.35 mV on vB2 changes J by 0.35/5 = 7%, so δφ ≈ 0.07π ≈ 0.22 rad rms, an infidelity of 0.15 × 0.22² ≈ 0.7%.

Dephasing of both qubits during 200 ns, with T2* = 6 µs: about 2 × ½(0.2/6)² ≈ 0.1%, less with an echo.

Total about 0.8%, close to the 99.3% we will measure in Chapter 26. The CZ is limited by charge noise on the exchange.

The best results show what is possible. QuTech's CZ, made with a cosine-window barrier pulse at the symmetry point, reached 99.65% by GST, and its residual errors were mostly incoherent . Princeton's processor exceeded 99% for its operations, and UNSW's benchmarks of two-qubit gates in SiMOS showed the same trends with their own platform .

The controlled rotation

The second route uses the split spectrum of Chapter 24 directly. With J switched on, qubit 1 has two resonance lines, one for each state of qubit 2. A microwave π pulse on only one of them flips qubit 1 if and only if qubit 2 is in the corresponding state. That is a controlled rotation, CROT, equivalent to a CNOT up to phases .

The difficulty is the other line, only J away. A pulse aimed at one line also drives the other, off resonance. That unwanted rotation vanishes if, during the π pulse, the off-resonant transition happens to complete a whole number of full turns. For a square pulse this happens when J/fR = √3, √15, √35, … (Figure 25.2). RIKEN chose J/fR = √15, with J = 18.85 MHz and fR = 4.867 MHz, giving a 103 ns CNOT with 99.51% fidelity by interleaved RB .

Princeton's first CNOT in silicon added another detail. While J is on, the conditional phase grows as in a CZ, so they applied a DC exchange pulse of duration 2π/J to bring it back to a multiple of 2π, and absorbed the single-qubit phases into the microwave phases. Their conditional π rotation took 130 ns, and the Bell-state fidelity of 75% was limited mostly by readout .

CROT needs microwaves during the two-qubit gate and a fixed, well-known J; CZ needs only a baseband pulse. Many groups use both: CZ for circuits, CROT for mapping a qubit onto a readout ancilla, as QuTech did for the inner qubits of their six-qubit device (Chapter 17) .

SWAP

If J is much larger than ΔEZ, the flip-flop part of exchange is no longer suppressed. A pulse with ∫J dt = ½ then swaps the two spins completely, a SWAP gate; half of that, √SWAP, is entangling. This was the original proposal for spin qubits, and in exchange-only qubits, where all operations are made from exchange pulses, SWAP is native: HRL's exchange-only processor reached 99.3% for SWAP . In a device like ours, a fast SWAP would need J well above 300 MHz. The alternative is to drive the swap resonantly, by modulating J at ΔEZ/h, the same idea RIKEN used to drive a singlet–triplet qubit with exchange pulses at its frequency . SWAP matters for moving quantum information through an array, a job that shuttling the electrons themselves can also do (Chapter 29).

When it goes wrong

What you see: the CZ conditional phase is calibrated to π, but a sequence of several CZ gates goes wrong quickly, and the error depends on how long ago the last calibration was.

Likely causes: J drifts, through slow charge rearrangement near the barrier, and with it the conditional phase. Or heating from the barrier pulses shifts the qubit frequencies, so single-qubit phases change with the number of gates.

What to do: recalibrate J(V) and the CZ phase frequently (Chapter 31). Check the conditional phase after 1, 5 and 11 CZ gates: an error that grows linearly is a calibration error; one that grows with the gate rate is heating.

Check yourself

1. A flat-top (Tukey) CZ pulse has 30 ns cosine ramps and a 100 ns flat top at J = 3 MHz. Is it a CZ?

Show answer
The integral is the flat top plus the two ramps, each of which contributes half its duration times J: 3 MHz × (100 + 30) ns = 0.39. That is less than ½, a conditional phase of 0.78π. Lengthen the flat top to 137 ns, or raise J to about 3.85 MHz.

2. Why must the CZ ramps be slow compared with h/ΔEZ, and what goes wrong if they are not?

Show answer
The eigenstates in the presence of exchange are slightly mixed antiparallel states. A sudden change of J projects the state onto the new eigenstates, leaving some amplitude in the "wrong" one, which then oscillates at ΔEZ/h: a partial swap of ↑↓ and ↓↑. Ramps slow compared with 1/(300 MHz) ≈ 3 ns, in practice tens of nanoseconds, let the states follow adiabatically.

3. In a CROT with fR = 2 MHz, what J satisfies the first two synchronization conditions?

Show answer
J = √3 × 2 MHz ≈ 3.46 MHz and J = √15 × 2 MHz ≈ 7.75 MHz.
