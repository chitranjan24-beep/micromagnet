A Rabi oscillation shows that the qubit can be rotated. A Ramsey experiment shows how well it keeps time when left alone, which is what a qubit does most of the time in any computation. It is the most precise way to measure the qubit frequency, and its decay, T2*, is the first measure of the noise that will set most of the error budget. This chapter explains the experiment, why its result depends on how long you measure, and how to follow the qubit frequency as it drifts.

Two π/2 pulses and a wait

A π/2 pulse puts the spin on the equator of the Bloch sphere. Left alone, it precesses about the field at the qubit frequency. In the rotating frame of the drive, it stands still if the drive is exactly on resonance, and turns slowly at the detuning Δ otherwise. After a wait τ, a second π/2 pulse rotates the equator back onto the poles. Where the spin ends up depends on the angle 2πΔτ it has turned through: the measured spin-up probability oscillates with τ at the detuning frequency, the Ramsey fringe .

\[ P_\uparrow(\tau) = \tfrac{1}{2}\left[1 + \cos(2\pi\Delta\tau)\,e^{-(\tau/T_2^*)^2}\right]. \]

The fringes are a clock comparison: the drive's frequency against the qubit's. They measure Δ to a precision of roughly 1/(2πτmax), far better than a spectroscopy line. To measure the qubit frequency, detune the drive on purpose by about 1 MHz, so that several fringes fit into the decay, and fit (Figure 20.1).

One subtlety: the fringe frequency gives |Δ|, not its sign. Whether the qubit is above or below the drive follows from a second measurement at a different intentional detuning, or by advancing the phase of the second pulse with τ, which adds a known frequency to the fringes. Delft used the phase of the second pulse as an independent check of their Ramsey data .

Why the fringes fade

Every shot of the experiment uses the qubit frequency at that moment. If the frequency fluctuates slowly from shot to shot by a random amount with standard deviation σf, each shot's fringe has a slightly different phase, and their average washes out once 2πσfτ approaches one. For Gaussian-distributed, quasi-static fluctuations the envelope is Gaussian, exp[−(τ/T2*)²], with

\[ T_2^* = \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2\pi\sigma_f}. \]

So T2* is not a property of a single shot, in which the spin hardly dephases, but of the spread of frequencies over the whole data set. Our 6 µs corresponds to σf ≈ 38 kHz, a frequency stability of 2.4 parts per million at 15.4 GHz. RIKEN's purified-silicon qubit reached T2* = 20 µs, with an envelope exponent of 2.0, the Gaussian shape expected for slow noise . The shape of the envelope is itself informative: an exponential decay instead points to noise that is fast compared to τ.

A dephasing time that depends on your patience

Where does the frequency spread come from? In our device mostly from charge noise, moving the electron in the micromagnet's longitudinal gradient (Chapter 14), with a smaller contribution from the remaining 29Si nuclei. Charge noise in these devices has a 1/f spectrum: equal noise power in every decade of frequency, down to the lowest frequencies anyone has measured . That has an unsettling consequence. A measurement that lasts longer includes slower fluctuations, so its frequency spread is larger and its T2* shorter. For 1/f noise, the variance grows with the logarithm of the total measurement time (Figure 20.2).

Worked numbers: logarithms and patience

For noise with spectral density S(f) = A/f between a lowest frequency flow ≈ 1/Tmeas and a highest frequency fhigh ≈ 1/τ, the variance is σf² = A ln(fhigh/flow).

Our numbers: A ≈ 87 kHz², fhigh ≈ 100 kHz. For a 100 s measurement, ln(105 × 100) ≈ 16, so σf = √(87 × 16) ≈ 38 kHz and T2* = 6 µs. For a 1 hour measurement, ln(3.6 × 108) ≈ 20, σf ≈ 42 kHz and T2* ≈ 5.4 µs.

A factor of 36 in time costs only 10% in T2*, which is why 1/f noise is often mistaken for "a fixed T2*", until a much longer or shorter experiment is done.

This is the main reason published T2* values are hard to compare, and why good papers state the averaging time. It also shows the way to better effective coherence. If the slow part of the noise could be measured and corrected as it happens, only the fast part would remain. The two ways of doing that, echo sequences and frequency tracking, are the subject of the rest of this chapter and of the next.

Following the frequency: Ramsey tracking

A single Ramsey measurement with a few hundred shots takes about a second and determines the frequency to a few kilohertz. Repeating it continuously turns the qubit into a sensor of its own environment (Figure 20.3). RIKEN recorded 3600 such measurements over 24 minutes and found that the same 1/f spectrum measured by echo experiments at high frequencies extended down to 0.01 Hz, seven decades in all . Such records also show events that averages hide: jumps when a nearby charge trap switches state, slow drifts from the superconducting magnet, and correlations between the frequencies of neighbouring qubits, which reveal whether the noise is local or shared .

Because the slow noise dominates, simply re-measuring the frequency and updating the drive removes much of it. Groups do this at different time scales. RIKEN recalibrated the microwave frequency by Ramsey measurements to follow the decay of the magnet's persistent current . Princeton retuned frequency and readout about every 30 minutes during 14-hour runs . QuTech's calibration graph re-runs the frequency calibration several times a day . At the fast end, the frequency can be estimated in real time on an FPGA from a few single shots and corrected within milliseconds, using Bayesian estimation that chooses each next measurement to be maximally informative. This has been demonstrated for the fluctuating nuclear field of GaAs and for silicon double dots . Chapter 31 puts these into a calibration strategy.

When it goes wrong

What you see: the Ramsey fringes are clean on one run and much shorter-lived on the next, or the fringe frequency changes between consecutive runs.

Likely cause: slow frequency jumps, often a charge trap near the dot switching between two states. Each run then catches one or both frequencies.

What to do: track the frequency continuously for an hour (Figure 20.3) to see the jumps directly. If a fluctuator switches often, a small change of gate voltages can move it out of its active range, or the dot away from it.

Check yourself

1. Ramsey fringes at a programmed detuning of +2 MHz oscillate at 2.05 MHz. With a programmed detuning of −2 MHz they oscillate at 1.95 MHz. Where is the qubit?

Show answer
The actual detunings are Δ = (programmed offset) − (qubit offset). If the qubit is δ above the nominal frequency, the fringes oscillate at |2 MHz − δ| and |−2 MHz − δ|. From 2.05 and 1.95 MHz: |2 − δ| = 2.05 needs δ = −0.05 MHz, and then |−2 + 0.05| = 1.95 ✓. The qubit is 50 kHz below the nominal frequency.

2. Why does a Ramsey experiment measure frequency far more precisely than a spectroscopy line of the same total duration?

Show answer
Ramsey interferometry compares phases accumulated over the full free evolution time, with the spin fully on the equator, the most phase-sensitive state. A spectroscopy line, especially a power-broadened one, spreads the measurement over a range of frequencies set by the drive. Ramsey's resolution is set only by the evolution time and the shot noise.

3. A colleague quotes T2* = 12 µs for a qubit like ours. What should you ask before comparing?

Show answer
How long the measurement took in total, and whether any frequency tracking or post-selection was applied. With 1/f noise, a fast measurement or one with frequent recalibration gives a longer T2*. Also ask about the field and the dot's position in the gradient, which change the noise coupling.
