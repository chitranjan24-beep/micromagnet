A step-by-step guide to semiconductor spin-qubit experiments

From Chip to Qubit

How a spin-qubit experiment is really done, from a bonded chip to benchmarked two-qubit gates, and why each step comes where it does

A spin-qubit experiment is a long chain of measurements. Each one answers a single question about the device, and each answer is a number you need for the next step. This book walks that chain from beginning to end on one realistic device, so that by the end you know not only what labs do, but why they do it in that order and what each plot is telling you.

Version 1.0, October 2026. Written for a reader with undergraduate physics, mathematics and solid-state physics. Facts and typical numbers are taken from the research papers listed at the end; where a number depends strongly on the device, the text says so.

The story this book follows

Almost everything here is told through one device, which we simply call our device. It is the kind of device that most silicon spin-qubit groups use today: two quantum dots in an isotopically purified 28Si/SiGe heterostructure, defined by three layers of overlapping gates, with a cobalt micromagnet on top and a third, larger dot next to them that serves as a charge sensor. We take it from the moment the chip arrives in the lab to the moment two-qubit gates are benchmarked. Along the way you will meet the same gates, the same lever arms and the same frequencies again and again, so the numbers become familiar rather than abstract.

Two other devices appear when they teach something the silicon device cannot. A GaAs double dot shows how the field began, why nuclear spins matter, and how singlet–triplet qubits work in their original setting. A silicon triple dot shows how exchange-only qubits are operated. Holes in germanium get their own chapter, because they are now one of the most active platforms.

The map of the book

How to read it

Read Parts A to G in order the first time. They are one continuous experiment, and later chapters lean on numbers measured earlier. Part H can be read in any order once you have finished Part F. Part I is about keeping a working device working, which is where most lab time actually goes.

A few recurring elements help you keep your bearings:

Device passport

At the end of most chapters, a short table lists the numbers we have now measured on our device and where each will be used next. By Part G the passport is a complete description of a working two-qubit processor. Real labs keep exactly this kind of record.

Lab notebook

Short entries written the way an experimentalist writes them at the end of a session: what was measured, the settings, what looked odd. They show the rhythm of real work, including the false starts.

When it goes wrong

Each measurement can fail in a handful of typical ways. These boxes pair what you see with its usual cause and what to try. Appendix C collects all of them into one troubleshooting index.

Worked numbers

Calculations with real values, worked through step by step so you can repeat them with your own device's numbers.

Check yourself

Questions at the end of a chapter, with answers you can open. Try them before opening the answers; they are chosen to test the reasoning, not memory.
