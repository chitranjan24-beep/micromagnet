How fast an electron can hop between a dot and its reservoir, and how strongly it is coupled to the neighbouring dot, decide almost everything that comes next. Spin readout needs the dot–reservoir rate in a narrow window. Pauli-blockade readout needs a suitable interdot coupling. The exchange interaction behind two-qubit gates is tunnelling in disguise. This chapter measures and sets these rates, and on the way introduces two powerful tools: the detuning axis and photon-assisted tunnelling.

The detuning axis

Zoom in on one interdot transition of the stability diagram, the one between (1,0) and (0,1). Moving along the transition line changes the total energy of both configurations together. Moving across it changes their energy difference. That difference is the detuning ε, and the direction in gate space that changes ε without changing the total is the detuning axis. With virtual gates it is simply vP2 − vP1, scaled so that ε is in units of energy:

\[ \varepsilon = e\,\alpha\,(\delta v_{P2} - \delta v_{P1}) \quad \text{(for equal lever arms } \alpha\text{)}. \]

Every operation near an interdot transition is described along this axis: initialization, readout, exchange. It is worth setting up a virtual detuning gate in the software, together with its orthogonal partner (both plungers moving together), so that pulses can be programmed directly in ε.

Tunnel coupling and the interdot anticrossing

If the two dots were not coupled at all, the electron would sit in dot 1 for ε < 0 and in dot 2 for ε > 0, with an abrupt switch at ε = 0, smeared only by temperature. With a tunnel coupling tc between them, the two charge states mix near ε = 0 into bonding and antibonding molecular states, separated by

\[ \Omega = \sqrt{\varepsilon^2 + 4t_c^2}. \]

The electron's position, which is what the sensor measures, then changes smoothly across a width set by tc (Figure 12.1). In thermal equilibrium the probability of finding it in dot 2 is

\[ P_2(\varepsilon) = \frac{1}{2}\left[1 + \frac{\varepsilon}{\Omega}\tanh\!\left(\frac{\Omega}{2k_BT_e}\right)\right]. \]

Fitting a sensor trace across the transition with this formula gives tc, as long as tc is not much smaller than kBTe. Then temperature dominates, and the fit only gives an upper bound. Larger couplings also visibly round the corners of the honeycomb cells, and HRL extracted tc from that curvature . For accurate values, especially in the range that matters for qubits, a frequency measurement is better.

Photon-assisted tunnelling

Apply a continuous microwave tone of frequency f to one of the plungers, and sweep the detuning. Wherever the photon energy hf matches the splitting Ω between the bonding and antibonding states, the microwaves drive the electron into the excited charge state, which sits mostly in the "wrong" dot. The sensor sees this as an extra peak (or dip) beside the interdot transition. Repeating at many frequencies traces out the dispersion Ω(ε) directly (Figure 12.2). This is photon-assisted tunnelling (PAT) .

PAT is the most precise way to measure the interdot coupling, and it does double duty. The asymptotes of the hyperbola give the lever arm along the detuning axis without any transport measurement. The same technique measures excited-state energies, such as valley splittings, which show up as additional resonance lines. HRL used PAT to measure valley energies of 70 and 14 µeV in two dots of their exchange-only device . It also checks, early on, that microwaves actually reach the gate they are applied to, which matters before you rely on that gate for EDSR (Chapter 18).

Dot–reservoir tunnel rates

The rate Γ at which an electron tunnels between a dot and its reservoir is controlled by the outer barrier, B1 for dot 1 and B3 for dot 2. It depends exponentially on the barrier voltage: HRL found the rate changing as exp(V/12.8 mV), one decade per about 30 mV, and could tune it over nine orders of magnitude with a single gate . Because of this exponential sensitivity, the rate must be measured, not guessed, and it must be measured at the configuration where it will be used: moving a plunger changes the barrier as well.

Three methods cover the whole range:

  1. Smearing of transition lines (slow rates). Apply a square wave of frequency f to the plunger while sweeping across a dot–reservoir transition. If Γ is much larger than f, the electron follows the square wave and the transition appears split into two copies. If Γ is smaller, it cannot follow and the lines blur. The crossover gives Γ to within a factor of a few, which is quick and useful for orientation .
  2. Averaged time-resolved response (kilohertz to megahertz). Pulse the dot level from above the Fermi level to below it and back, and record the sensor signal after each edge, averaged over thousands of repetitions. The occupation relaxes exponentially, and fitting gives the loading and unloading rates (Figure 12.3).
  3. Single-shot dwell times (rates below the sensor bandwidth). Park the level at the Fermi level and record the random telegraph signal (Chapter 9). Histograms of the times spent in each state are exponential, with time constants 1/Γin and 1/Γout.

Different stages of the experiment want very different rates, which is why the barriers are re-tuned (or pulsed) between stages:

Interdot coupling versus barrier

The interdot tunnel coupling, controlled by the middle barrier B2, is just as exponential: HRL measured tc ∝ exp(VX/10.1 mV) for their interdot gate . In our device, tc runs from below 1 µeV ("off") to well over 100 µeV over 60 mV of vB2 (Figure 12.4, right). That range matters for two reasons. For Pauli-blockade readout (Chapter 16), tc must be large enough for the singlet to tunnel quickly into (0,2), typically 10 to 50 µeV. For two-qubit gates, the exchange interaction J ≈ 4tc²/U, with U the charging energy, must be switchable from almost zero to megahertz, which requires exactly this exponential control (Chapter 24). The virtual barrier vB2, which changes tc without moving either dot, is the knob for both.

Pulsed-gate spectroscopy of excited states

One more measurement in the same family finds a dot's excited states without bias, which is useful once transport no longer works. Apply a square wave to the plunger that alternately lifts the dot level above the Fermi level and drops it below. With a small amplitude, only the ground state drops below the Fermi level, and electrons load into it at its tunnel rate. When the amplitude is large enough to pull an excited state below the Fermi level too, a second loading channel opens and the average sensor signal changes. The pulse amplitude at which that happens, times the lever arm, is the excited-state energy. Princeton measured orbital energies of about 3 meV in each of their nine dots this way . The same idea, with spin states instead of orbitals, will be the basis of the spin readout in Chapter 15.

When it goes wrong

What you see: the PAT map shows no resonance, although the sensor clearly sees the interdot transition.

Likely causes: the microwaves are not reaching the gate (a broken or badly attenuated line, a bias tee that blocks high frequencies, a bad bond); the power is far too low; or the tunnel coupling is so large that the gap 2tc/h lies above your frequency range.

What to do: raise the power gradually and look for broadening of the interdot line, which shows that some microwave power arrives. Check the line's transmission at room temperature in the next warm-up. Lower tc with vB2 to bring the gap into range.

1. A PAT hyperbola has its minimum at 6 GHz. What is tc, in µeV and in GHz?

Show answer
2tc/h = 6 GHz, so tc/h = 3 GHz, which is 3 × 4.136 µeV ≈ 12.4 µeV.

2. The dot–reservoir rate is 2 kHz, and you need 30 kHz. With one decade per 30 mV, how much must the barrier change? What else must you adjust?

Show answer
A factor of 15 is log₁₀15 ≈ 1.18 decades, so about 35 mV more on the barrier. The barrier also pulls on the dot level (about 30% of a plunger in our device), so the plunger must be lowered by roughly 10 mV to keep the level in place. With a virtual barrier, this compensation is automatic.

3. Why does the width of the interdot transition stop telling you tc when tc is very small?

Show answer
When 2tc is much smaller than kBTe, the thermal smearing of the occupation is wider than the tunnel-coupling broadening, so the transition width reflects the electron temperature, not tc. PAT, which measures the gap directly as a frequency, remains accurate down to much smaller couplings.
