Everything up to now has been about charge: where the electrons are and how fast they move. The qubit, though, is the electron's spin, and a spin only becomes a usable two-level system once a magnetic field splits its two states. This chapter turns on the field. It explains how strong it should be and why, what silicon's valleys do to the picture, why the spin relaxes and how that depends on the field, and what the micromagnet adds and costs. By the end you will know what to expect when you first look for a spin signal, and why the numbers in the device passport are what they are.

The Zeeman splitting and the energy scale it must beat

In a magnetic field B, the two spin states of an electron differ in energy by

\[ E_Z = g\,\mu_B\,|\mathbf{B}|, \qquad \frac{E_Z}{h} = \frac{g}{2}\times 27.99\ \text{GHz/T}. \]

For electrons in silicon, g is very close to 2, so the splitting is 28 GHz per tesla, 116 µeV per tesla. In GaAs, g ≈ −0.44 and the splitting is only 6.2 GHz per tesla; the negative sign means the spin-up state is the lower one there . The field our electron sees is the sum of the external field from the superconducting magnet, 0.40 T, and the stray field of the micromagnet, about 0.15 T, so the qubit in dot 1 sits at 15.42 GHz, and its two states are 64 µeV apart.

The field is chosen inside a window. Too low, and readout through the reservoir fails thermally; groups that read out by Pauli blockade instead can go lower (Chapter 16). Too high, and the spin relaxes faster, eventually running into the valley hotspot; the microwave electronics also get harder above 20 GHz. Most micromagnet devices in silicon therefore work at 0.3–0.6 T of total field, qubit frequencies of about 10–20 GHz: Princeton's readout work used 0.41 T and 19.1 GHz, RIKEN's CROT experiments 0.41 T, QuTech's six-qubit device about 15.6–16 GHz, and QuTech's two-qubit GST work about 12 GHz . GaAs, with its small g-factor, needed several tesla for the same EZ/kBT: Elzerman's single-shot readout used 10 T .

Valleys: the extra degree of freedom in silicon

An electron in the conduction band of bulk silicon can sit in any of six equivalent energy minima, the valleys. The tensile strain of the quantum well lifts four of them, leaving two. The sharp interface of the well then mixes and splits those two by the valley splitting EVS. Each orbital state of the dot therefore comes as a valley pair, and the lowest four levels for a single electron are the two valley states, each split by the Zeeman energy (Figure 14.1).

EVS is the number in a Si/SiGe device that varies most, and it matters a lot. In Si/SiGe it is typically tens to a few hundred µeV, and it can differ by a factor of several between neighbouring dots on the same chip. The reason is partly the atomic-scale disorder of the SiGe alloy at the interface: each dot samples a different random arrangement of Ge atoms . Silicon MOS devices, with an abrupt Si/SiO2 interface and strong vertical fields, usually have larger and gate-tunable splittings, up to several hundred µeV . A small EVS causes three distinct problems:

  • Thermal occupation. If EVS is only a few kBT, the electron is sometimes loaded into the upper valley, which behaves like a different qubit with a slightly different g-factor. Delft's first micromagnet qubit in silicon showed two EDSR lines a few megahertz apart for exactly this reason .
  • Pauli-blockade readout. The singlet–triplet energy gap in the doubly occupied dot is set by EVS when it is small. A small gap makes the blockade weak or absent (Chapter 16).
  • Spin relaxation. Near EZ = EVS, the spin relaxes through the upper valley, the hotspot of Figure 14.1.

So EVS is measured early, for every dot. Pulsed-gate spectroscopy and photon-assisted tunnelling (Chapter 12) show it as an excited-state line. Measuring T1 versus field locates the hotspot, which gives EVS = gµBBhotspot with high precision; this is how Jülich found valley splittings above 200 µeV in a Si/SiGe dot . A rule of thumb from the table above: EVS should be at least about 1.5 times EZ and many times kBTe. Our dot 2, with 120 µeV, is adequate; a dot with 30 µeV would not be.

Spin relaxation and how it depends on the field

A spin-up electron eventually relaxes to spin-down, releasing EZ as a phonon. A bare spin cannot couple to the lattice vibrations, because phonons are electric (strain) fields. The coupling comes from small admixtures: spin–orbit interaction, which mixes a little orbital character into the spin states, and in our device also the micromagnet's gradient, which does the same thing artificially. The relaxation time is therefore long, but it falls steeply as the field rises. The phonon density of states and the coupling both grow with energy, giving rates that rise as B5 to B7 depending on the mechanism . At the valley hotspot the admixture becomes large and T1 collapses by orders of magnitude (Figure 14.2). At very low fields, T1 levels off, limited by electrical noise rather than phonons.

T1 at the operating point is one of the first spin numbers you will measure, in Chapter 15, and it sets two things. It bounds the readout error, because a spin-up electron that relaxes before it can tunnel out is read as spin-down. Princeton's 31.5 ms at 0.41 T contributed about 0.2% to their readout error . It is also a quick health check. A T1 much shorter than expected usually means EVS is smaller than you thought, or that electrical noise reaches the dot.

Nuclear spins: why isotope purification matters

The electron also feels the magnetic moments of the nuclei it overlaps with. Natural silicon contains 4.7% of the isotope 29Si, which has a nuclear spin; 28Si has none. The few thousand 29Si nuclei inside the electron's wavefunction add a small, slowly fluctuating effective field, the Overhauser field. Its random part, about 10 µT in a natural-silicon dot, makes the qubit frequency wander by a few hundred kilohertz and limits the dephasing time T2* to about a microsecond . Purified silicon with 800 ppm of 29Si, as in our device, reduces this by a factor of about eight in field, and T2* rises to tens of microseconds, at which point charge noise takes over as the limit . In GaAs every nucleus has a spin, the Overhauser field is millitesla, and T2* is about 10 ns. That difference is the main reason the field moved from GaAs to silicon, and it shapes how GaAs qubits are operated (Chapter 27). Chapter 20 returns to dephasing in detail.

The micromagnet: what it gives and what it costs

A spin does not respond to an electric field. But a spin that moves through a spatially varying magnetic field sees a changing field. The cobalt micromagnet on top of our device creates exactly such a variation, and it does three jobs :

  1. Driving. A microwave voltage on a gate shakes the electron back and forth by a tiny amount. In the transverse gradient btrans = ∂B⊥/∂x, of about 0.3–1 mT/nm, this motion becomes an oscillating transverse magnetic field at the qubit frequency, which rotates the spin: electric-dipole spin resonance, or EDSR (Chapter 18) .
  2. Addressing. The longitudinal field differs between the two dots, so their qubit frequencies differ, here by 300 MHz. A microwave tone at one frequency drives one qubit and leaves the other alone. The same frequency difference makes the exchange interaction act as a controlled phase gate (Chapter 24).
  3. Setting the operating field. The magnet adds about 0.15 T, which lets the external field be smaller. But this only works if the magnet is fully magnetized along the field direction, which requires an external field of a few tenths of a tesla.

The cost is that the gradient also turns electric noise into magnetic noise. Charge fluctuations in the device move the electron by tiny amounts, and in the longitudinal gradient blong = ∂B∥/∂x every movement shifts the qubit frequency.

Worked numbers: how far does the electron move?

Our design has blong ≈ 0.2 mT/nm. A displacement of 10 pm, a twentieth of the Si–Si bond length, changes the field by 0.2 mT/nm × 0.01 nm = 2 µT, and the qubit frequency by 28 GHz/T × 2 µT = 56 kHz.

With T2* = 6 µs, the qubit's frequency fluctuates by about √2/(2π T2*) ≈ 37 kHz rms. If charge noise were the only cause, the electron's position would be wandering by only about 7 pm rms.

Delft's natural-silicon device had a similar gradient and T2* = 0.84 µs, which bounded the electron's motion at about 50 pm rms . RIKEN's design kept blong at about 0.2 mT/nm while raising btrans to about 1 mT/nm, and estimated that blong must stay well below 3 mT/nm for their orbital energy and charge noise .

Good micromagnet design is therefore a trade-off. The transverse gradient should be large for fast driving, the longitudinal gradient at the dot small for coherence, the difference in longitudinal field between the dots large for addressing, and all of this robust to a misalignment of the magnet by tens of nanometres. Designs that place each dot at a point where the longitudinal gradient is flat achieve this .

Measuring the field your qubit actually sees

The design simulation in Chapter 2 predicted the stray field to within some tens of percent, and it predicted a frequency difference of about 0.5 GHz between the dots, where we will measure 300 MHz. The real numbers come from the qubits themselves. Once the resonance has been found (Chapter 18), measure the qubit frequency of each dot at a few external fields (Figure 14.3). Above the field where the magnet saturates, the frequency rises linearly. The slope gives g, and the intercept at zero external field gives the micromagnet's longitudinal field at that dot.

Two practical consequences follow. Change the field only in one direction once the qubits are tuned, and always arrive at the operating field the same way, because below saturation, and to some extent above it, the magnet's field depends on its history. And keep the superconducting magnet in persistent mode, where the field is very stable but still drifts slowly. RIKEN recalibrated the qubit frequency regularly using Ramsey measurements for this reason . A drift of one part per million at 0.4 T is 0.4 µT, or 11 kHz, which is a noticeable fraction of the 37 kHz frequency noise we just estimated.

When it goes wrong

What you see: T1 is only a few hundred microseconds at a field where you expected tens of milliseconds, and it gets dramatically worse as the field is raised.

Likely cause: a small valley splitting, so that the operating field sits near the hotspot.

What to do: measure T1 at several fields to find the hotspot, then EVS = gµBBhotspot. Lower the field, or move the dot slightly with the gates: EVS can change by tens of percent as the dot moves over a different patch of interface . If EVS is below about 50 µeV, consider using a different dot.

Check yourself

1. A GaAs dot at Te = 100 mK needs EZ ≥ 13 kBTe for good Elzerman readout. What field is that?

Show answer
13 × 8.6 µeV = 112 µeV. With |g| = 0.44, EZ = 0.44 × 57.9 µeV/T × B = 25.5 µeV/T × B, so B ≈ 4.4 T. This is why GaAs Elzerman readout was done at several tesla.

2. A dot shows a T1 hotspot at 0.62 T of total field. What is its valley splitting, and is a qubit at 15 GHz a good idea in this dot?

Show answer
EVS = 115.8 µeV/T × 0.62 T ≈ 72 µeV. A 15 GHz qubit has EZ = 62 µeV, only 14% below the hotspot, so T1 would be short. Lower the field to about 0.35 T (10 GHz, 41 µeV), if readout still allows it, or retune the dot's position to change EVS.

3. Why does increasing the transverse gradient help, while increasing the longitudinal gradient at the dot hurts?

Show answer
The transverse gradient converts the electron's driven motion into a field perpendicular to the quantization axis, which rotates the spin: more gradient, faster gates for the same electric drive. The longitudinal gradient converts unwanted motion, from charge noise, into changes of the field along the quantization axis, which changes the qubit frequency and dephases it.
