Why you need this

Singlet–triplet and exchange-only qubits are controlled entirely by baseband pulses (Chapter 13), so they are the hardest fit for the 4x2's AC-coupled outputs and the clearest test of what your setup can do. This chapter describes the standard experiments for both encodings, what each requires from the hardware, and what part a 4x2 can play: full control in a modified two-channel configuration for S–T₀, readout and calibration support for exchange-only devices run from a larger controller.

Singlet–triplet qubit experiments

Hardware needs

  • Two fast, DC-coupled baseband channels: P1 and P2 (detuning control) or a plunger and the barrier (symmetric exchange), with sub-ns timing and ~ns rise times.
  • PSB readout: a sensor carrier and receiver.
  • A stable field difference \(\Delta B_z\) between the dots (micromagnet in Si).

With the 4x2, use Architecture A (AWG for P1/P2 with the 4x2 reading out) or Architecture B (DC-coupled DAC_A and DAC_B, readout carrier from an external source) (Chapter 38).

The experiments

  1. S–T₀ precession (ΔBz oscillations): initialise \(S(2,0)\), pulse quickly (diabatically with respect to \(\Delta E_Z\)) deep into \((1,1)\) where \(J\approx0\), wait \(\tau\), return and read. The singlet probability oscillates at \(\Delta E_Z/h\), giving the field difference and, from the decay, \(T_2^*\) of the S–T₀ qubit.
  2. Exchange oscillations: prepare \(\ket{\uparrow\downarrow}\) (adiabatic separation), apply an exchange pulse of amplitude \(\varepsilon\) and duration \(\tau\), reverse the adiabatic ramp, read. The outcome oscillates at \(\sqrt{J^2+\Delta E_Z^2}/h\). A 2D map versus pulse amplitude and duration is the "fingerprint" from which \(J(\varepsilon)\) is calibrated .
  3. Exchange echo: a π pulse about \(\Delta B_z\) in the middle of an exchange evolution refocuses slow detuning noise, measuring charge-noise-limited coherence of the exchange axis.
  4. Two-axis gates: combine exchange pulses and \(\Delta B_z\) evolution for arbitrary rotations; benchmark with RB.
Worked example: timing resolution

At the top of the fingerprint, \(J/h \approx 150\) MHz: a full oscillation every 6.7 ns. The 4x2's DAC sample period is 0.1 ns, and QICK envelopes are sample-accurate, so a pulse's duration can be set in 0.1 ns steps inside an envelope even though the tProcessor's scheduling grid is 2.44 ns. A 0.1 ns error is then a 5° rotation error at 150 MHz, acceptable for calibration scans and improvable with smaller \(J\) for precise gates.

Exchange-only qubit experiments

HRL's RFSoC-based work on a triangular EO qubit is the reference for this encoding on QICK hardware . The sequence of experiments was:

  1. Tune a triple dot to the \((1,1,1)\) region with PSB readout on one pair.
  2. Exchange oscillations on each axis (\(J_{12}\), \(J_{23}\), and \(J_{13}\) for the triangle), with crosstalk-compensated pulses so that pulsing one barrier does not shift the dots. Reported: \(J/h\approx 75\)–78 MHz and 21–35 oscillations before 1/e decay.
  3. Fingerprint plots versus exchange-pulse amplitude and detuning for each axis, to set the symmetric exchange operating point.
  4. Pre-distortion of 10 ns exchange pulses using a measured response of the amplifier chain, and a calibrated 40 ns gap between pulses.
  5. Exchange-angle calibration, then dynamical-decoupling sequences (NZ1 and the new NZM1 enabled by the triangular geometry) to separate hyperfine and charge-noise contributions.

Hardware: a ZCU216 with 14 baseband channels and 2 DDS channels, DC-coupled LMH5401 and THS3491 amplifiers, an 11 × 11 crosstalk matrix in firmware and a 20-bit DC source .

On the 4x2

An EO qubit needs at least two exchange (barrier) channels and usually plunger channels for initialisation and readout, all DC-coupled: more than the 4x2's two AC-coupled outputs. Use a ZCU216-based system (or a commercial controller) for EO control. A 4x2 can still serve as a dedicated readout unit, a calibration instrument (photon-assisted tunnelling, sensor characterisation, noise spectroscopy with its ADCs and DDR4 buffer), or a development platform for QICK programs before running them on the larger board, since QICK's programming model is the same across boards .

Check yourself

1. In the S–T₀ precession experiment, why must the pulse into (1,1) be fast compared with \(h/\Delta E_Z\)?

Show answer
So that the singlet state is preserved as it enters (1,1) and then precesses towards T₀ under \(\Delta E_Z\). A slow (adiabatic) pulse would instead map the singlet onto an eigenstate of \(\Delta E_Z\) (\(\ket{\uparrow\downarrow}\) or \(\ket{\downarrow\uparrow}\)), and no precession would be seen.

2. Why does an EO qubit benefit so much from crosstalk compensation in firmware?

Show answer
Each exchange pulse on a barrier also shifts neighbouring dot potentials (and other exchange couplings) through cross-capacitance, changing the qubit's operating point during the pulse. Compensating pulses on neighbouring gates, computed from a crosstalk matrix, keep the pulse acting only on the intended exchange axis. Doing it in firmware keeps programs simple and timing exact.
Summary
  • S–T₀ experiments: ΔBz precession, exchange oscillations and fingerprints, exchange echo, two-axis gates; they need two fast DC-coupled channels plus PSB readout.
  • EO experiments (HRL/spinQICK): crosstalk-compensated exchange on each axis, fingerprints, pre-distorted 10 ns pulses, exchange calibration and decoupling sequences, on a ZCU216 with 14 baseband channels.
  • The 4x2 can run S–T₀ control only with an AWG or a DC-coupling modification; for EO it is a readout, calibration or development unit.
