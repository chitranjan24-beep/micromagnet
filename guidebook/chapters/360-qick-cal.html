Why you need this

Before the qubit can tell you anything, the controller must be calibrated: when does a pulse actually arrive at the ADC, what power comes out of each DAC at each frequency, how do generator and readout phases relate, how large is a pulse at the device, how late is an external instrument. Uncalibrated offsets look like physics (a shifted resonance, a "decay" of contrast, a wrong Rabi frequency). This chapter lists the calibrations an RFSoC-based spin-qubit setup needs, how to do each, and how often.

1. Loopback and output power

What: the power each DAC delivers versus frequency and gain, and what each ADC reports for a known input.

How: DAC → spectrum analyser (or power meter) for output power; DAC → known attenuator → ADC for the loopback response. Sweep frequency across your bands, in the Nyquist zones you will use. Record DAC output in dBm versus QICK gain at your working frequencies.

Why: every power budget (Chapter 18) starts from these numbers. Zone-2 power in particular varies strongly with frequency (Chapter 19).

When: once, then after firmware or hardware changes.

2. Time of flight

What: the delay between a generator pulse starting and its arrival in the readout data, through converters, FPGA pipelines and cables.

How: play a short pulse and record a decimated trace with the readout triggered at t = 0 (as in Chapter 34's loopback). The pulse appears after a delay: that is the time of flight. Repeat with the real fridge cabling, since metres of cable add 5 ns per metre. Then set your readout trigger time to this delay plus the resonator ring-up time.

When: once per wiring configuration. QICK's documentation includes a dedicated page on timing and the tutorials show how pulse and trigger times relate .

3. Frequency matching

What: ensuring the readout's demodulation frequency equals the generator's carrier frequency exactly. Generators and readouts run on different clocks, so their frequency grids differ; a mismatch of a few Hz rotates the demodulated phase slowly, making each run's I/Q land at a different angle .

How: pass ro_ch to add_pulse() and gen_ch to add_readoutconfig(), or round the frequency first with soccfg.adcfreq(f, gen_ch=..., ro_ch=...). On the 4x2, the generator's own step is 2.3 Hz and the common step 20.6 Hz; without matching, a 100 MHz readout would be off by about 9 Hz, rotating the phase once every 0.1 s .

When: always, in every program. It costs nothing.

4. Phase offset between generator and readout

What: even with matched frequencies, the demodulated signal has a fixed phase offset that depends on the frequency (through the time of flight: phase = 360° × f × delay) and on the state of the clock dividers after power-up. QICK's older demo describes this as a constant phase difference "randomly determined each time the board clocks are initialized" .

How: measure the loopback (or the reflected signal off-resonance) phase versus frequency; the slope gives the electrical delay, the intercept the offset. Subtract it, or simply rotate the IQ plane as part of readout calibration (Chapter 23).

When: after each power cycle if you rely on absolute phase. For charge-state readout, rotating the IQ plane on your measured data (two charge states) does the job automatically.

5. Readout working point

What: carrier frequency, power, integration window and IQ rotation that maximise SNR for your sensor.

How: sweep carrier frequency across the tank resonance with the sensor on and off a Coulomb peak; choose the frequency of maximum difference. Sweep power to find where the signal stops growing without heating (Coulomb peaks broaden). Record single-shot data for the two charge states and choose the window length and threshold (Chapter 23).

When: after tuning the sensor; re-check daily, since sensor drift changes the optimum.

6. Amplitude at the device

What: the actual voltage a gate sees for a given QICK gain (or AWG amplitude), after attenuation, reflections and frequency-dependent losses.

How, for baseband pulses: apply a square pulse of known nominal amplitude to a plunger and observe how far a charge transition shifts in a CSD measured during the pulse; with the lever arm this gives the real amplitude. For microwaves: photon-assisted tunnelling side-peaks spaced by \(hf/(e\alpha)\) give the amplitude at frequency \(f\) through their strength (Bessel-function dependence), and the measured Rabi frequency versus gain gives the drive's linear range.

When: once per cooldown and per frequency band.

7. Channel skew and external trigger latency

What: relative delays between DAC_A and DAC_B outputs (different tiles and paths), and between a QICK PMOD trigger and an external AWG's output.

How: send simultaneous edges on all channels into a fast oscilloscope with matched cables; measure offsets. For the external AWG, trigger it from a PMOD pin and measure the delay from the PMOD edge (or from a reference QICK pulse) to the AWG output. Compensate in the QICK program (shift times) or in the AWG sequence.

When: once per configuration; re-check if you change instruments or cables. Lock everything to the same 10 MHz reference to keep jitter low (Chapter 30).

8. IQ mixer calibration (if you upconvert)

What: LO leakage and the unwanted sideband from an external IQ mixer.

How: with a spectrum analyser at the mixer output, adjust small DC offsets on I and Q to minimise LO leakage, then adjust the relative amplitude and phase of I and Q to minimise the image. With a double-sideband mixer and a filter, check the filter's suppression instead.

When: after any change of LO frequency, IF or power; re-check weekly, since offsets drift with temperature.

9. Pulse distortion

What: the impulse response of the baseband chain (amplifiers, filters, bias tees, cables), needed for pre-distortion (Chapter 21).

How: measure the step response of the room-temperature chain with a sampling oscilloscope, as HRL did for their spinQICK setup ; fit a small number of filter terms; verify on the qubit (for example with exchange-pulse sequences whose result depends on pulse shape).

When: once per hardware configuration.

In the lab

Keep calibrations as code and data, not as notes: a Python file or configuration object with every number (time of flight, phase offsets, gains per dBm, trigger latencies), version-controlled and saved with every dataset. spinQICK uses typed configuration models for this purpose . Six months later, when a result looks strange, you will want to know exactly what the calibration was.

Check yourself

1. Your demodulated phase changes by 36° when you move the readout from 200 MHz to 210 MHz. What electrical delay does that imply?

Show answer
Phase slope = 360° × Δf × τ, so τ = 36°/(360° × 10 MHz) = 10 ns (modulo full turns: the true delay could be 10 ns + n × 100 ns; measure at finer frequency steps to resolve the ambiguity).

2. Why does an unmatched generator/readout frequency pair cause problems only between runs, not within one run?

Show answer
A few-Hz mismatch rotates the phase by only a tiny amount during a µs readout, so within a shot it is invisible, but over seconds between runs it accumulates to large angles, so different runs' I/Q values appear rotated relative to each other.
Summary
  • Calibrate output power, time of flight, frequency matching, phase offsets, readout working point, device amplitudes, channel skew and trigger latency, IQ mixers and pulse distortion.
  • Frequency matching is a one-line habit; phase offsets change at power-up; device amplitudes need the qubit device itself.
  • Store calibrations as versioned data next to your measurements.
