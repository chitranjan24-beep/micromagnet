Why you need this

A qubit experiment is a timing problem. The phase of every microwave pulse, the start of every readout window and the trigger of every external instrument must be fixed relative to each other, shot after shot, to well below a nanosecond and a degree. Inside the RFSoC this is easy, because everything runs from one clock tree. Between the 4x2 and the rest of your lab (an external LO, an AWG, a DC source, a second board) it takes deliberate work. This chapter explains the board's clock tree, how to lock it to a lab reference, how QICK keeps time, and how to trigger and synchronise other instruments.

The clock tree on the 4x2

Because the DACs, ADCs and fabric all derive from one reference, a generator and a readout on the same board are always frequency-locked and keep a fixed phase relationship while the board is running. QICK's documentation calls this "an advantage of the RFSoC": all converter clocks are synthesised from a common reference and are always phase-locked with respect to each other . After each power-up, though, the fixed phase offsets between different tiles and between generator and readout can change, which is why phase-sensitive experiments calibrate them after every boot (Chapter 36).

QICK programs the clock chips the first time you create a QickSoc after power-on. On the 4x2, four LEDs labelled "CLOCK STATUS" light up and should stay on steadily .

Locking to a lab reference

Use an external reference when you need better frequency stability (QICK's documentation notes that touching the on-board oscillator's can shifts a DAC tone by hundreds of Hz) or when the 4x2 must be phase-locked to other instruments, such as the LO for EDSR upconversion . On the 4x2:

  • Feed a 10 MHz reference into the CLK IN SMA and create the SoC with QickSoc(..., external_clk=True) .
  • The input goes without attenuation to an AC-coupled single-ended LMK04828B input. The LMK data sheet asks for roughly 0.35–2.4 V peak-to-peak; QICK's documentation suggests 0 to +10 dBm as a safe range .
  • The usual lab setup: a rubidium or OCXO 10 MHz distribution amplifier feeding the 4x2, the microwave LO, the AWG and any other instrument that must stay phase-locked.
Common mistake

Locking the 4x2 and the EDSR local oscillator to different references, or one of them to nothing. Two free-running oscillators drift relative to each other by Hz to kHz. The upconverted drive's phase then wanders relative to QICK's virtual-Z bookkeeping, which shows up as decaying Ramsey or echo contrast that looks like decoherence but is not.

How QICK keeps time

The tProcessor schedules every event (pulse starts, readout triggers, PMOD outputs) at an absolute time on a counter that runs from a fabric timing clock. In tProc v2 this is a 48-bit absolute time counter shared by all outputs . The 4x2 standard firmware's printout reports a tProc timing clock of 409.6 MHz (2.44 ns per tick) . Events are placed on this grid; within one generator, pulse waveforms themselves are defined sample by sample at the full DAC rate (0.1 ns), so fine delays can be built into the envelope.

Different outputs have different fixed latencies through their data paths. QICK and spinQICK provide calibrations for these offsets; measure them once with an oscilloscope or a loopback, and correct in software (Chapter 36).

Triggers to and from other instruments

  • Outputs: QICK drives 8 PMOD pins (PMOD0_0–PMOD0_7) as timed digital outputs from the tProcessor, with the same timing grid as the pulses . Use them to trigger AWGs, DC sources, oscilloscopes, or a laser. They are 1.8 V logic (Chapter 28): add a level shifter for 3.3 V/5 V inputs.
  • Input: an external start pin (PMOD1_0 in the 4x2 firmware) can start a tProcessor program on an external event .
  • Latency and jitter: a trigger from the 4x2 to an external AWG has a fixed delay (cables, the AWG's trigger latency, often tens to hundreds of ns) and some jitter. Lock both instruments to the same 10 MHz to reduce jitter, measure the delay with a scope, and shift the QICK timeline to compensate.

Synchronising several boards

Larger experiments need more channels than one board has. QICK supports multi-board operation: a SYNC input on the 4x2's LMK allows clock-divider synchronisation across boards (the manual notes that LMK outputs OUT1 and OUT5 must be enabled for this) , and QICK's tutorials cover multi-board synchronisation and the XCOM system, a full-mesh synchronisation and low-latency communication network between QICK boards . Multi-board synchronisation is more mature on the ZCU216-based systems that large QICK users run.

Check yourself

1. Why is the phase between a QICK generator and readout stable within a session but possibly different after a reboot?

Show answer
Both derive from one reference and are phase-locked while running, but tile PLLs, dividers and data-path FIFOs can start in different states at each power-up, giving a new fixed offset. Hence recalibration after boot.

2. What do you connect, and how do you call QICK, to lock the 4x2 to your lab rubidium standard?

Show answer
A 10 MHz output of the standard (0 to +10 dBm) into CLK IN, and QickSoc(..., external_clk=True).
Summary
  • Si5395, LMK04828 and two LMX2594s generate all clocks from one reference; converters and fabric are always mutually phase-locked.
  • Lock to a lab reference with 10 MHz on CLK IN and external_clk=True; lock the LO and other instruments to the same reference.
  • QICK schedules events on a ~2.44 ns tProcessor grid (4x2 standard firmware); waveforms are sample-accurate at 0.1 ns.
  • PMOD0_0–7 are timed trigger outputs (1.8 V); PMOD1_0 can start programs; measure and compensate trigger latencies.
  • Multi-board synchronisation uses SYNC IN and QICK's synchronisation tools such as XCOM.
