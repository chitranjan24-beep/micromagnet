Why you need this

Before learning any single topic in depth, you need a mental map of the whole experiment: what the device is, why it sits in a refrigerator colder than outer space, what kinds of electrical signals it needs, which instrument makes each signal, and where the RFSoC 4x2 fits. Every later chapter zooms into one box of the map drawn here. If you ever feel lost later, come back to Figure 1.1.

A spin-qubit experiment is, at its heart, a conversation between a computer and a single electron. The computer sends carefully timed voltages and microwaves down wires into a refrigerator. Those signals move the electron around, rotate its spin, and then ask it a question: "is your spin up or down?" The answer comes back as a tiny change in an electrical signal, which is amplified, digitised and analysed. This chapter walks through that conversation once, end to end, in plain language.

What the lab is trying to do

A qubit is a physical object with two distinguishable quantum states, which we call \(\ket{0}\) and \(\ket{1}\). Unlike a classical bit, it can also be in a superposition of both. Chapter 2 explains what that means precisely. For now, think of it like this: a classical bit is a switch that is either off or on. A qubit is more like an arrow that can point in any direction on a sphere, where "north" means \(\ket{0}\) and "south" means \(\ket{1}\).

Many physical systems can play this role: superconducting circuits, trapped ions, neutral atoms, defects in diamond, photons, and the system this book is about, the spin of an electron (or hole) trapped in a semiconductor quantum dot. An electron has an intrinsic angular momentum called spin. In a magnetic field its two spin states, "up" and "down", have slightly different energies, and that pair of states is a natural qubit. This idea was proposed by Daniel Loss and David DiVincenzo in 1998 and is now called the Loss–DiVincenzo or single-spin qubit .

Why semiconductors? Three reasons dominate the research literature :

  • Size. A quantum dot is about 50–100 nm across. Billions could fit on a chip, much like transistors.
  • Manufacturing. Silicon spin qubits are made with processes close to those that make computer chips, so the semiconductor industry's know-how can, in principle, be reused.
  • Coherence. In isotopically purified silicon (28Si), the electron's spin is very well isolated from its surroundings, so its quantum state survives a long time compared with how fast we can manipulate it.

The lab's job is to build such devices, cool them down, find the right voltages to trap exactly one or a few electrons, and then demonstrate and improve the basic operations: preparing a known state, rotating the spin (single-qubit gates), coupling two spins (two-qubit gates), and measuring the spin (readout). Everything else in this book serves those four tasks.

The device: a chip with gates on top

Figure 1.1 shows a simplified cross-section of a Si/SiGe quantum-dot device, the platform you will use. Read it from the bottom up:

  1. Substrate and buffer. Germanium atoms are slightly larger than silicon atoms. A thick, graded SiGe layer slowly changes the lattice spacing so that the top layers grow as a nearly perfect crystal.
  2. The quantum well. A thin (about 10 nm) layer of pure silicon is sandwiched between SiGe layers. Because of how the conduction band energies line up (Chapter 6), electrons prefer to sit in this silicon layer. They can move freely in the plane but not up or down, so they form a two-dimensional electron gas (2DEG).
  3. Spacer and oxide. A SiGe spacer keeps the electrons away from the messy surface, and a thin oxide insulates the gates from the semiconductor.
  4. Gates. Metal electrodes, a few tens of nanometres wide, sit on top. A voltage on a gate changes the electrostatic potential underneath it. Positive voltage attracts electrons, negative repels them. With the right combination of voltages you carve the 2DEG into tiny islands (dots) that each hold a controlled number of electrons, typically one.
  5. Micromagnet. A small ferromagnet (often cobalt) on top of the device. Chapter 12 explains why its non-uniform stray field is so useful.

GaAs devices look similar but use a GaAs/AlGaAs interface instead of a Si quantum well. In GaAs the 2DEG often forms by itself (thanks to doping) and the gates are negative, pushing electrons away to define dots. Chapter 6 compares the two materials, including why GaAs spins lose their coherence faster (nuclear spins) and why silicon has an extra complication called valleys.

Why the device must be so cold

The energies involved are tiny. The energy needed to add one more electron to a dot (the charging energy) is a few milli-electronvolts (meV). The energy difference between spin up and spin down in a 1 T field is about 0.1 meV. Thermal energy at temperature \(T\) is about \(k_B T\), where \(k_B = 86\ \mu\text{eV/K}\) is Boltzmann's constant. At room temperature (300 K), \(k_B T \approx 26\) meV, far larger than all of these energies, so thermal jiggling would wash everything out.

Worked example

What temperature makes thermal energy 10 times smaller than the spin splitting at 0.5 T?

The spin splitting is \(E_Z = g \mu_B B\), with \(g \approx 2\) for electrons in silicon and \(\mu_B = 57.9\ \mu\text{eV/T}\) (the Bohr magneton). At \(B = 0.5\) T: \(E_Z = 2 \times 57.9 \times 0.5 = 57.9\ \mu\text{eV}\). We want \(k_B T = E_Z / 10 = 5.8\ \mu\text{eV}\), so \(T = 5.8 / 86 \approx 0.067\) K = 67 mK.

That is why spin-qubit devices sit in a dilution refrigerator, whose coldest stage (the mixing chamber) reaches about 10 mK. The electrons themselves usually end up somewhat hotter (often 50–150 mK) because of electrical noise coming down the wires. Chapter 16 explains how the wiring is designed to minimise this.

Interestingly, there is a strong research push towards running silicon spin qubits at 1 K or above ("hot qubits"), where much more cooling power is available. The physics is the same; the trade-offs are discussed in Chapter 16.

Where the RFSoC 4x2 fits

The RFSoC (Radio-Frequency System-on-Chip) is a single chip made by AMD (formerly Xilinx) that contains four things that used to be separate:

  1. Fast DACs (digital-to-analog converters) that turn numbers into voltages billions of times per second. On the 4x2 they run at up to 9.85 billion samples per second (GSPS).
  2. Fast ADCs (analog-to-digital converters) that turn voltages into numbers, up to 5 GSPS on this chip.
  3. FPGA fabric: millions of programmable logic elements that can be wired into custom digital circuits, for example a circuit that generates a pulse with nanosecond timing, or one that demodulates a readout signal in real time.
  4. ARM processors that run Linux, so the board is a small computer you can talk to over Ethernet.

Originally designed for 5G base stations and radar, this combination turns out to be very close to what a qubit controller needs. The QICK project (Quantum Instrumentation Control Kit), started at Fermilab and Princeton, provides open-source firmware and Python software that turn an RFSoC board into a qubit controller . HRL Laboratories has published spinQICK, an extension of QICK made specifically for gate-defined spin qubits , and demonstrated full control of an exchange-only qubit with it in October 2026 .

Here is how one "shot" of a typical spin-qubit experiment flows through Figure 1.1:

  1. You write a short Python program in a Jupyter notebook. With QICK it might say: "pulse gate P1 to −5 mV for 1 µs (initialise), apply a 20 ns microwave burst at 5.2 GHz (rotate the spin), pulse P1 to the readout point and record the sensor signal for 2 µs; repeat 10 000 times."
  2. The QICK library compiles this into a program for the tProcessor, a small processor built in the FPGA fabric that executes commands at precise clock ticks, and sends it to the board over Ethernet.
  3. The tProcessor tells the signal generators exactly when to start. The generators compute waveform samples and feed them to the DACs at 9.8 GSPS.
  4. Analog signals leave the SMA connectors, pass through room-temperature electronics and down the fridge lines to the device.
  5. The electron responds. Its spin state is converted into a charge state (Chapter 11), which changes the conductance of a nearby charge sensor, which changes the readout signal reflected back up the fridge.
  6. The ADC samples the returning signal. FPGA logic multiplies it by a reference sine wave, filters and adds up the result, so that each shot gives just two numbers, I and Q (Chapter 23).
  7. After all repetitions the averaged or single-shot I/Q values are sent back to the PC, where you plot them, fit them and decide what to change.

On the 4x2

The most important single fact for your work is the third row. Spin qubits in gate-defined dots are controlled mostly by baseband gate voltages, meaning pulses that start and end at a DC level, last anything from nanoseconds to milliseconds, and have no carrier frequency. The 4x2's outputs are transformer-coupled and AC-coupled, so they cannot hold a DC level. The experiments that used RFSoCs on spin qubits so far all used the larger ZCU216 board with DC-coupled amplifiers . With the 4x2 you can still do a great deal: RF reflectometry readout, microwave EDSR drive, fast charge-stability maps using AC-coupled tricks, single-shot readout analysis and real-time feedback. Baseband pulsing needs extra hardware, discussed honestly in Chapters 29 and 38.

The software stack, from top to bottom

Several layers of software and firmware sit between your notebook and the DAC pins. You will meet each in its own chapter, so here is the list:

  1. Jupyter notebook: an interactive Python document in your browser (Chapter 31).
  2. Your experiment code: Python classes describing pulse sequences, sweeps and analysis. With spinQICK, many standard spin-qubit experiments are already written.
  3. QICK Python library: translates your pulse sequence into tProcessor machine code, configures generators and readouts, and collects data (Chapters 33–35).
  4. PYNQ: AMD's Python framework that loads FPGA designs ("overlays") and gives Python access to their registers and memories (Chapters 31–32).
  5. Linux on the ARM cores.
  6. The bitstream: the file that configures the FPGA fabric into QICK's circuits (Chapter 27).
  7. The RF data converters: hard-wired DAC and ADC blocks on the chip, configured by the bitstream and by Python (Chapter 26).
Analogy

Think of the RFSoC as an orchestra. The ARM CPU is the manager who talks to the outside world (you) and hands out the sheet music. The tProcessor in the FPGA is the conductor who keeps perfect time. The signal generators are the musicians who play the notes, and the DACs are their instruments. The ADCs and readout blocks are the recording engineers. QICK is the arrangement that tells everyone their part. PYNQ is the stage crew who set up the hall.

Check yourself

1. Why can't spin-qubit experiments be done at room temperature?

Show answer
Thermal energy \(k_BT\) at 300 K (about 26 meV) is much larger than the charging energy (a few meV) and the spin splitting (about 0.1 meV at 1 T), so thermal excitations would randomise both the electron number and the spin. Cooling to about 10–100 mK makes \(k_BT\) of order 1–10 µeV, well below these energies.

2. Name the four kinds of electrical signal most spin-qubit experiments need, and say which one the 4x2 cannot produce without modification.

Show answer
DC gate voltages, fast baseband gate pulses, microwave drive, and readout stimulus/response (plus triggers). The 4x2 cannot produce DC voltages or long baseband pulses because its outputs are AC-coupled through capacitors and baluns.

3. In one sentence each, what do the DAC, ADC, FPGA fabric and ARM CPU of an RFSoC do in a qubit experiment?

Show answer
The DAC turns computed waveform samples into the analog pulses sent to the device; the ADC digitises the returning readout signal; the FPGA fabric runs the timing processor, waveform generators and real-time demodulation; the ARM CPU runs Linux and Python, receives programs from the PC and returns data.
Summary
  • A spin qubit is the spin of a single electron (or hole) trapped in a gate-defined quantum dot, cooled to millikelvin temperatures in a dilution refrigerator.
  • Experiments need DC gate voltages, fast baseband pulses, microwave drive, readout signals and triggers.
  • An RFSoC combines fast DACs, fast ADCs, FPGA logic and ARM CPUs; QICK and spinQICK turn it into a qubit controller.
  • The 4x2 has 2 outputs and 4 inputs, all AC-coupled (10 MHz–10 GHz baluns). It is excellent for readout and microwave work and needs extra hardware for baseband gate pulsing.
