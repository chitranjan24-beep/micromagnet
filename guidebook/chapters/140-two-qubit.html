Why you need this

A quantum computer needs entangling gates. For spins they all come from the exchange interaction, switched on for tens of nanoseconds by a barrier-gate pulse while single-qubit microwave pulses correct phases and rotate targets. Programming a two-qubit gate means synchronising a shaped baseband pulse, one or more microwave bursts and a readout, all to within a nanosecond, which is the most demanding thing you will ask of a controller. This chapter explains the physics behind the three standard gates and what their pulses look like.

Two spins with exchange and different Zeeman energies

Two LD qubits in neighbouring dots, with Zeeman energies \(E_{Z1}\) and \(E_{Z2}\) (different, thanks to the micromagnet), coupled by exchange \(J\):

\[ H = \frac{E_{Z1}}{2}\sigma_z^{(1)} + \frac{E_{Z2}}{2}\sigma_z^{(2)} + \frac{J}{4}\big(\vec\sigma^{(1)}\cdot\vec\sigma^{(2)} - 1\big). \]

Write \(\Delta E_Z = E_{Z1} - E_{Z2}\). Two regimes give two different kinds of gate:

  • \(J \ll \Delta E_Z\): the flip-flop part of exchange (\(\sigma_x\sigma_x + \sigma_y\sigma_y\)) connects \(\ket{\uparrow\downarrow}\) and \(\ket{\downarrow\uparrow}\), which differ in energy by \(\Delta E_Z\). It is strongly off-resonant and has almost no effect. What remains is the Ising part \(\tfrac J4\sigma_z\sigma_z\): a controlled phase.
  • \(J \gg \Delta E_Z\): exchange dominates and swaps the two spins: a SWAP gate.

Silicon micromagnet devices typically have \(\Delta E_Z/h\) of tens to hundreds of MHz and operate in the first regime.

The CZ (controlled-phase) gate

In the regime \(J\ll\Delta E_Z\), the four computational states stay (nearly) product states, and their energies are

\[ E_{\uparrow\uparrow} = \bar E_Z,\quad E_{\downarrow\downarrow} = -\bar E_Z,\quad E_{\uparrow\downarrow} = \tfrac{\Delta E_Z}{2} - \tfrac{J}{2},\quad E_{\downarrow\uparrow} = -\tfrac{\Delta E_Z}{2} - \tfrac{J}{2}, \]

with \(\bar E_Z = (E_{Z1}+E_{Z2})/2\). The antiparallel states are lowered by \(J/2\) each. Over a pulse during which exchange is on, the four states pick up phases; subtracting the single-qubit parts (which a virtual Z on each qubit can undo) leaves a conditional phase

\[ \phi_{CP} = \frac{1}{\hbar}\int J(t)\,dt . \]

When \(\phi_{CP} = \pi\), the gate is CZ = diag(1, 1, 1, −1) up to single-qubit Z rotations. For a constant \(J\), the gate time is \(t_{CZ} = h/(2J)\).

Worked example

With \(J/h = 5\) MHz during the pulse, \(t_{CZ} = 1/(2\times5\ \text{MHz}) = 100\) ns. With \(\Delta E_Z/h = 100\) MHz, \(J/\Delta E_Z = 0.05\): small enough that the swap-like error is small, provided the pulse is turned on and off smoothly. Silicon experiments typically use CZ gates of 50–200 ns.

Why the pulse shape matters

Turning \(J\) on suddenly excites unwanted transitions between \(\ket{\uparrow\downarrow}\) and \(\ket{\downarrow\uparrow}\), because the eigenstates change abruptly. A smooth envelope (for example a cosine or Hann window) keeps the evolution adiabatic with respect to \(\Delta E_Z\), so the states follow the slowly changing eigenstates and only acquire phase. The silicon experiments that crossed 99% two-qubit fidelity all used carefully shaped barrier pulses and calibrated the single-qubit phase corrections . A further subtlety: the barrier pulse also shifts the dot potentials slightly through cross-capacitance, so the plungers are compensated with virtual-gate pulses at the same time.

The CROT (conditional rotation) gate

While \(J\) is on, the frequency of qubit 2 depends on the state of qubit 1: from the energies above, qubit 2's transition is at \(f_2 \pm J/(2h)\). The spectrum splits into four lines.

A microwave π pulse at one of the four frequencies, long enough to resolve the \(J\) splitting (pulse bandwidth smaller than \(J/h\)), flips the target only for one control state. This is a CROT, equivalent to a CNOT up to single-qubit phases. It needs exchange on during a frequency-selective microwave pulse, so both baseband and microwave channels must be synchronised.

The SWAP gate

When \(J \gg \Delta E_Z\), the eigenstates of the two spins become singlet and triplets, and a pulse with \(\int J\,dt/\hbar = \pi\) exchanges the two spin states: \(\ket{\uparrow\downarrow}\to\ket{\downarrow\uparrow}\). The √SWAP (half the pulse area) is entangling and universal together with single-qubit gates. SWAP gates are also useful to move quantum information along a chain without moving electrons. They need large, fast exchange pulses, and residual \(\Delta E_Z\) causes errors unless the pulse is short or the field difference is small.

Where the field stands

In 2022, three groups demonstrated two-qubit gates in silicon above 99% fidelity: Delft (Xue et al.), RIKEN (Noiri et al.) and Princeton (Mills et al.), all in Si/SiGe with micromagnets . Delft operated a six-qubit processor with universal control the same year . HRL demonstrated universal logic between exchange-only encoded qubits . The open questions now are mostly about scale: uniformity of many qubits, crosstalk, calibration effort, wiring and control electronics. That last point is exactly where open-source RFSoC controllers come in.

Scaling beyond nearest neighbours

  • Linear arrays: 4–12 dots in a line, each with plunger and barrier gates, read by sensors at the sides .
  • 2D arrays: 2×2 and larger, with more complex gate stacks.
  • Shuttling: moving electrons coherently along a channel with a travelling potential, to link distant qubit regions. Shuttling with "conveyor-belt" gates uses several phase-shifted sinusoids at MHz frequencies on a set of gates, a natural task for multi-channel DACs.
  • Spin–photon coupling: coupling spins to a superconducting microwave resonator, for long-range interactions.
On the 4x2

A minimal CZ or CROT experiment on two LD qubits needs at least:

  • one microwave channel (EDSR for both qubits, frequency-multiplexed),
  • one DC-coupled fast baseband channel for the barrier (exchange) pulse, plus plunger compensation,
  • one or two baseband channels for the PSB readout pulse on the plungers,
  • one readout carrier and one ADC.

That is three to five DAC channels; the 4x2 has two, both AC-coupled. Realistically, a two-qubit experiment uses the 4x2 for readout and microwaves together with a DC-coupled AWG for the gate pulses, or moves to a larger board. Chapter 44 discusses how to synchronise them and what the 4x2 can still contribute, such as real-time feedback and calibration loops.

Check yourself

1. Why does a CZ gate require \(J \ll \Delta E_Z\), while a SWAP requires \(J\gg\Delta E_Z\)?

Show answer
When \(\Delta E_Z\) dominates, the flip-flop term is off-resonant, so exchange only adds a state-dependent energy shift (an Ising coupling), giving a controlled phase. When \(J\) dominates, the flip-flop term is resonant and exchanges the spin states.

2. \(J/h = 2\) MHz. How long is the CZ gate? How long must a CROT π pulse be, at least, to resolve the J-split lines?

Show answer
\(t_{CZ} = 1/(2\times2\ \text{MHz}) = 250\) ns. For the CROT, choose the Rabi frequency so that the unwanted line (detuned by \(J/h\)) completes a whole number of full rotations while the wanted line gets exactly π. For a square pulse this "synchronisation" condition gives \(f_R = (J/h)/\sqrt{3} \approx 1.15\) MHz (π pulse ≈ 430 ns) or, with more margin, \(f_R = (J/h)/\sqrt{15} \approx 0.52\) MHz (π pulse ≈ 1 µs).
Summary
  • All spin two-qubit gates use exchange \(J\), switched by barrier-gate pulses.
  • \(J\ll\Delta E_Z\): CZ with \(\phi_{CP} = \int J\,dt/\hbar = \pi\); smooth pulses keep it adiabatic.
  • With exchange on, qubit frequencies split by \(J/h\): a selective microwave π pulse makes a CROT (CNOT).
  • \(J\gg\Delta E_Z\): SWAP and √SWAP.
  • Silicon passed 99% two-qubit fidelity in 2022; scaling now depends heavily on control electronics.
