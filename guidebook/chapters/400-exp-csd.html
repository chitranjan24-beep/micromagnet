Why you need this

Charge stability diagrams are the map you navigate by (Chapter 8), and you will take thousands of them. With DC readout each takes minutes; with RF readout and fast scanning, a map can refresh several times per second, so you can tune gates while watching ("video mode"). Pulsed-gate spectroscopy then adds time resolution: it measures tunnel rates and excited-state energies by pulsing a gate during the scan. This chapter shows how to build both with the 4x2 as the measuring and timing engine, and an AWG or a triggered DC source doing the sweeping.

Video mode in detail

Design rules:

  • Ramp rate versus bandwidth: each pixel needs a dwell time longer than the readout's response time (tank ring-down plus filtering). For a 100-pixel row with 2 µs per pixel, the ramp lasts 200 µs (5 kHz). A 100-row map then takes 20 ms.
  • AC coupling of the fast axis: a sawtooth at 5 kHz passes a PCB bias tee (with a sub-Hz corner) almost undistorted but does not pass the 4x2's 10 MHz baluns. So the fast axis comes from a DC-coupled AWG, not from the 4x2's DACs.
  • Hysteresis and latency: charge transitions lag the gate slightly (tunnel times), and the readout chain delays the signal. Sweep in both directions or calibrate the pixel offset.
  • Recording: either one QICK readout per pixel (accumulated mode, one trigger per pixel, clean timing), or one long decimated record per row into the DDR4 buffer, binned in software.
Worked example: how fast can a map be?

With \(t_{\min} = 0.125\ \mu\)s (Chapter 39), a pixel SNR of 3 needs \(9\times0.125 \approx 1.1\ \mu\)s. A 128 × 128 map has 16 384 pixels: about 18 ms of integration. Overheads (ramp flyback, settling) may double it: about 25 maps per second. The same map with DC current readout at 10 ms per point would take 2.7 minutes.

Turning maps into virtual gates

Fast maps make it practical to calibrate the virtual-gate matrix automatically: detect transition lines in the image (edge detection or a Hough transform), fit their slopes, build the lever-arm matrix (Chapter 8), and rescan in virtual coordinates to check that dot-1 lines are vertical and dot-2 lines horizontal. Machine-learning classifiers that identify charge states and transitions from such images are reviewed by Zwolak and Taylor . spinQICK includes cross-capacitance extraction and gate-vs-gate scan routines .

Pulsed-gate spectroscopy

Add a square-wave pulse of amplitude \(V_p\) to a plunger during a 1D or 2D scan. When the pulse frequency is low compared with the tunnel rate, a charge transition appears twice, split by \(V_p\) (the dot sees two gate voltages alternately). As the pulse frequency approaches the tunnel rate, the split lines fade, because the electron cannot follow: the fading frequency measures the tunnel rate. Excited orbital or valley states appear as extra lines between the split copies, because during the high half of the pulse an electron can tunnel into an excited state if it is below the reservoir's Fermi level; their position gives the excitation energy . This is how valley splittings of individual dots are often first estimated.

On the 4x2

Square pulses at kHz–MHz on a plunger need a DC-coupled source (AWG), because the 4x2's outputs only pass edges. A useful variant that the 4x2 can do itself is photon-assisted tunnelling: a microwave tone (hundreds of MHz to GHz) from DAC_A, through a bias tee onto a plunger, creates side-peaks of charge transitions spaced by \(hf/(e\alpha)\). Measuring the spacing versus frequency calibrates the lever arm, and the side-peak strength calibrates the microwave amplitude at the gate (Chapter 36). spinQICK has a dedicated routine (gvg_pat) for this .

Check yourself

1. Your video-mode map shows transitions shifted between left-to-right and right-to-left sweeps. Why?

Show answer
Finite tunnel rates and the readout chain's response time make the measured transition lag the gate voltage. The lag reverses with sweep direction. Sweep slower, average both directions, or calibrate and correct the pixel offset.

2. In pulsed-gate spectroscopy, the split transition lines fade when the pulse period drops below 20 µs. What is the tunnel rate, roughly?

Show answer
Each half-period is 10 µs; the electron can no longer follow when the tunnel time is comparable, so \(\Gamma \sim 1/(10\ \mu\text{s}) = 100\) kHz (to within a factor of order one; fit the fading curve for a precise value).
Summary
  • Scan point by point, by triggered raster, or in video mode; the 4x2 measures and triggers, an AWG or DC source sweeps.
  • Video mode with RF readout gives full maps in tens of milliseconds; design pixel dwell time from \(t_{\min}\).
  • Fast maps enable automated virtual-gate calibration and machine-learning tuning.
  • Pulsed-gate spectroscopy measures tunnel rates and excited states; photon-assisted tunnelling (doable from the 4x2) calibrates lever arms and microwave amplitudes.
