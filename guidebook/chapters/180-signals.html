Why you need this

From here on, the book moves from physics to electronics. Every cable you connect to the RFSoC carries a signal described by amplitude, frequency and phase, measured in dBm, flowing through 50 Ω coax into components with gains and losses in dB. If you can do power budgets in your head, you can predict what reaches the device, avoid damaging the ADC, and understand data sheets. This chapter covers the vocabulary every RF engineer uses, following the fundamentals chapters of AMD's SDR book and standard microwave engineering .

Sine waves: amplitude, frequency and phase

The basic signal of RF engineering is the sinusoid

\[ v(t) = A\cos(2\pi f t + \phi) = A\cos(\omega t + \phi). \]
  • \(A\) is the amplitude (peak voltage). The peak-to-peak value is \(2A\) and the RMS value is \(A/\sqrt2\).
  • \(f\) is the frequency in Hz; its inverse \(T = 1/f\) is the period; \(\omega = 2\pi f\) is the angular frequency in rad/s.
  • \(\phi\) is the phase in radians or degrees: a shift of the wave along the time axis by \(\phi/\omega\).

Why sinusoids? Because linear systems (cables, filters, amplifiers in their linear range) do not change the frequency of a sinusoid; they only change its amplitude and phase. Any signal can be written as a sum of sinusoids (Fourier analysis, Chapter 21), so knowing a system's effect on each frequency tells you its effect on everything.

Power, decibels and dBm

The average power a sinusoid delivers into a resistance \(R\) is

\[ P = \frac{V_{\text{rms}}^2}{R} = \frac{A^2}{2R} . \]

RF power spans enormous ranges (from watts to below a femtowatt), so it is expressed logarithmically. A ratio of powers in decibels is \(10\log_{10}(P_2/P_1)\). For voltages (or currents) across the same resistance, power goes as voltage squared, so the voltage ratio in dB is \(20\log_{10}(V_2/V_1)\). The unit dBm is power relative to 1 mW:

\[ P_{\text{dBm}} = 10\log_{10}\frac{P}{1\ \text{mW}} . \]

Rules of thumb: +3 dB doubles power, +10 dB multiplies it by 10, +6 dB doubles voltage, +20 dB multiplies voltage by 10. Gains and losses in a chain simply add in dB, which is why engineers love them. Related units you will meet:

  • dBc: power relative to the carrier (for spurs and harmonics, e.g. "−60 dBc").
  • dBFS: power relative to a converter's full-scale signal (for ADC/DAC specs).
  • dBm/Hz and dBFS/Hz: noise power per hertz of bandwidth.

Impedance

For sinusoidal signals, resistors, capacitors and inductors are all described by a complex impedance \(Z = V/I\) (engineers write \(j\) for \(\sqrt{-1}\)):

\[ Z_R = R, \qquad Z_C = \frac{1}{j\omega C}, \qquad Z_L = j\omega L . \]

A capacitor blocks DC (infinite impedance at \(\omega = 0\)) and passes high frequencies; an inductor does the opposite. That is the whole idea behind bias tees, filters and the reflectometry tank circuit of Chapter 9.

Worked example

A 100 nF DC-blocking capacitor in series with a 50 Ω load forms a high-pass filter with cut-off \(f_c = 1/(2\pi RC) = 1/(2\pi\times50\times10^{-7}) \approx 32\) kHz. Below 32 kHz, the capacitor's impedance exceeds 50 Ω and the signal is increasingly blocked. That is why AC-coupled paths cannot pass slow signals or long flat pulses. The 4x2's SMA paths contain two 100 nF capacitors and a balun (Chapter 29); the balun limits the low-frequency end far more than the capacitors do.

Transmission lines and why everything is 50 Ω

At high frequencies, a cable longer than a small fraction of a wavelength is not a simple wire. It is a transmission line: signals travel along it as waves at about two-thirds of the speed of light (roughly 5 ns per metre in typical coax), and the ratio of voltage to current in a travelling wave is the line's characteristic impedance \(Z_0\), fixed by its geometry. Almost all RF equipment uses \(Z_0 = 50\) Ω, a historical compromise between low loss and high power handling in air-filled coax.

When a wave meets a load with impedance \(Z \ne Z_0\), part of it reflects, with reflection coefficient \(\Gamma = (Z - Z_0)/(Z + Z_0)\) (Chapter 9). Three cases matter for your lab:

  • Matched load (\(Z = 50\) Ω): no reflection. Instruments and attenuators are designed for this.
  • Open end (\(Z\to\infty\)), for example a fast gate line ending on a tiny gate capacitance: \(\Gamma = +1\). The voltage at the open end is twice the incident wave's voltage, and the reflected wave travels back up the line.
  • Short (\(Z = 0\)): \(\Gamma = -1\); the voltage at the end is zero.
Common mistake

Assuming the pulse amplitude at the gate equals the source's nominal 50 Ω amplitude. A gate at the end of a coax is essentially an open circuit at pulse frequencies, so the voltage there is up to twice the incident amplitude (after the line's attenuation), and reflections bounce between gate and source, ringing the pulse. Many groups put a 50 Ω termination or a resistive divider on the sample PCB, or calibrate the real amplitude with the qubit or with charge transitions. Always calibrate pulse amplitude at the device.

Differential signals and baluns

The RFSoC's converters are differential: each DAC output and ADC input is a pair of pins (P and N) carrying opposite voltages, and the signal is their difference. Differential signalling rejects noise that appears equally on both pins. Lab equipment, however, is single-ended: one coax centre conductor relative to ground. A balun (balanced–unbalanced transformer) converts between the two. Transformer baluns are coupled magnetically, so they cannot pass DC, and they work only over a specified frequency range. On the 4x2, each SMA connector reaches the chip through decoupling capacitors and a MACOM MABA-011118 balun rated from 10 MHz to 10 GHz . This one component explains most of the 4x2's limits for spin qubits.

Power budgets

A power budget follows the signal from source to destination, adding gains and subtracting losses in dB. Do one for every new setup.

Thermal noise and noise temperature

A resistor at temperature \(T\) delivers thermal noise power \(P_n = k_BTB\) into a matched load in a bandwidth \(B\). At 290 K this is −174 dBm per hertz, a number worth memorising: in a 1 MHz bandwidth, −174 + 60 = −114 dBm. Amplifiers add noise, described by a noise temperature \(T_N\) (the temperature of a resistor at the input that would produce the same added noise). For a chain of amplifiers with gains \(G_i\) and noise temperatures \(T_i\), the total is given by the Friis formula:

\[ T_{\text{sys}} = T_1 + \frac{T_2}{G_1} + \frac{T_3}{G_1G_2} + \dots \]

The first amplifier dominates when its gain is large. That is why a cryogenic HEMT (\(T_1\) of a few K, \(G_1\) about \(10^3\)–\(10^4\)) at 4 K makes a room-temperature amplifier's 100 K or more nearly irrelevant, and why the HEMT sets the readout SNR .

Worked example: readout SNR in 1 µs

The sensor's signal at the device is a change of −110 dBm in reflected power between charge states (a 10 fW difference). The HEMT has \(T_N = 4\) K. Noise power in a bandwidth of 1 MHz (about a 1 µs integration) is \(k_BT_NB = 1.38\times10^{-23}\times4\times10^6 = 5.5\times10^{-17}\) W = −132.6 dBm. The SNR in power is \(-110 - (-132.6) = 22.6\) dB, a factor of about 180 in power, or about 13 in amplitude. Plenty for single-shot readout, which is why a good HEMT at 4 K is essential.

On the 4x2
  • All four ADC and two DAC SMA ports are 50 Ω single-ended (through the baluns) .
  • DAC output power is set by the DAC's current mode (QICK's printout for the 4x2 reports "20 mA" mode) and by the digital amplitude (QICK gain, 0 to 1). The maximum output of a Gen3 RF-DAC is of order 0 dBm, and on Gen3 devices it can be adjusted in fine steps with Variable Output Power (VOP) . Measure the actual power at your frequency with a spectrum analyser before relying on a number.
  • The ADC full-scale input is of order +1 dBm; Gen3 ADCs also include a digital step attenuator in front of the converter . Check DS926 for your device's absolute maximum ratings .
Check yourself

1. Convert −40 dBm into amplitude in a 50 Ω system.

Show answer
−40 dBm = 100 nW. \(V_{\text{rms}} = \sqrt{PR} = \sqrt{10^{-7}\times50} = 2.24\) mV, amplitude \(= 3.16\) mV.

2. A chain has a DAC at 0 dBm, −20 dB, −20 dB, −10 dB attenuators and a −20 dB coupler. What power reaches the device?

Show answer
0 − 20 − 20 − 10 − 20 = −70 dBm.

3. Why does a balun block DC?

Show answer
It is a transformer: energy is transferred between windings by a changing magnetic flux, which requires a changing current. A constant (DC) current produces no changing flux, so nothing is coupled.
Summary
  • A sinusoid has amplitude, frequency and phase; power into \(R\) is \(A^2/2R\).
  • dB for ratios (10 log power, 20 log voltage), dBm relative to 1 mW; 0 dBm = 316 mV amplitude in 50 Ω.
  • Capacitors block DC, inductors block high frequencies; transmission lines have a characteristic impedance (50 Ω) and reflect at mismatches; an open gate sees up to twice the incident voltage.
  • The RFSoC's differential converters reach the 4x2's SMA ports through baluns, which block DC.
  • Thermal noise is −174 dBm/Hz at 290 K; the first amplifier (HEMT) sets the system noise.
