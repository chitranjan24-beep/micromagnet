Why you need this

The RFSoC is a direct RF-sampling device: its ADCs and DACs work directly at GHz frequencies, without the analog mixers older radios needed. To use it well, you must understand what sampling does to a signal: which frequencies the DAC can produce, where its unwanted images appear, which input frequencies an ADC confuses with each other, and how many effective bits you get. When QICK asks for nqz=1 or nqz=2 when you declare a generator, this chapter tells you what you are choosing .

Sampling a signal

An ADC measures the input voltage at regular instants \(t_n = nT_s\), where \(T_s = 1/f_s\) is the sampling period and \(f_s\) the sample rate. The result is a list of numbers \(x[n] = x(nT_s)\). A DAC does the reverse: it turns a list of numbers into a voltage that changes at each sample instant.

The sampling theorem (Nyquist–Shannon) says: a signal containing only frequencies below \(f_s/2\) is completely determined by its samples. The frequency \(f_s/2\) is the Nyquist frequency. Conversely, frequencies above \(f_s/2\) cannot be told apart from frequencies below it.

Aliasing

Sample a cosine at frequency \(f\) and another at \(f' = f_s - f\). At the sample instants,

\[ \cos(2\pi f' nT_s) = \cos(2\pi n - 2\pi f nT_s) = \cos(2\pi f n T_s), \]

so the two produce identical samples. More generally, all frequencies \(|f \pm kf_s|\) for integer \(k\) produce the same samples. This is aliasing.

The frequency axis is divided into Nyquist zones of width \(f_s/2\): zone 1 from 0 to \(f_s/2\), zone 2 from \(f_s/2\) to \(f_s\), zone 3 from \(f_s\) to \(3f_s/2\), and so on. After sampling, every zone folds onto zone 1. Even zones are mirrored (frequency increasing in zone 2 appears decreasing in zone 1).

Worked example: where does it fold?

The QICK readout on the 4x2 samples at \(f_s = 4423.68\) MSPS, so the Nyquist frequency is 2211.84 MHz. An input at 3000 MHz is in zone 2 and appears at \(4423.68 - 3000 = 1423.68\) MHz. An input at 5000 MHz is in zone 3 and appears at \(5000 - 4423.68 = 576.32\) MHz. If your readout carrier is at 576 MHz, a stray 5 GHz signal (for example leakage from a microwave line) would land on top of it. This is why analog filters before ADCs matter.

Using aliasing on purpose: RF sampling

Aliasing is not always bad. If you know your signal is in, say, zone 2, and you filter out everything else, the ADC can digitise it directly and the folding is just a known frequency mapping. This is RF sampling (or undersampling), and it is how the RFSoC's ADCs can digitise signals above their Nyquist frequency, up to their analog input bandwidth (about 6 GHz for the 4x2's ADCs ). For reflectometry at a few hundred MHz you do not need it: you work in zone 1.

The DAC side: zero-order hold, sinc roll-off and images

A DAC holds each sample's voltage for one sample period. Mathematically, the output is the ideal sampled signal convolved with a rectangular pulse of width \(T_s\). In frequency, convolution becomes multiplication, and the Fourier transform of a rectangle is a sinc. So the DAC's output spectrum is the ideal spectrum multiplied by

\[ |H(f)| = \left|\frac{\sin(\pi f/f_s)}{\pi f/f_s}\right| = |\operatorname{sinc}(f/f_s)| . \]

Two consequences :

  • Roll-off: output power falls with frequency, by about 3.9 dB at \(f_s/2\). Inverse-sinc digital filters can compensate in zone 1.
  • Images: a DAC playing a tone at \(f\) also produces images at \(f_s - f\), \(f_s + f\), \(2f_s - f\), …, weighted by the sinc. These must be filtered if they could reach the device.

Gen3 RF-DACs also offer mix-mode: each sample period is split into two halves of opposite sign, which moves the output response's maximum into zone 2. Normal mode is best in zone 1, mix-mode in zone 2 .

On the 4x2

In QICK, declare_gen(ch, nqz=1) tells the firmware and library that you will play frequencies in zone 1 (0 to 4915 MHz at the 4x2's 9830.4 MSPS DAC rate); nqz=2 sets the RF-DAC's Nyquist-zone mode to 2, which (as the QICK source notes, referring to AMD's PG269) increases output power in the 2nd and 3rd Nyquist zones; this is the mix-mode described above . Zone 2 spans 4915 to 9830 MHz. Expect lower and frequency-dependent power in zone 2, and measure it. The DAC rate of 9830.4 MSPS is 20 times the 491.52 MHz reference clock, and the ADC rate of 4423.68 MSPS is 9 times it .

Quantisation: how many bits you really get

An \(N\)-bit converter represents the voltage with \(2^N\) levels. The step between levels is one LSB \(\Delta = V_{\text{FS}}/2^N\). Rounding to the nearest level adds a quantisation error between \(-\Delta/2\) and \(+\Delta/2\). For a busy signal this error behaves like white noise with power \(\Delta^2/12\).

For a full-scale sinusoid, the ratio of signal power to quantisation noise power is

\[ \text{SNR}_{\text{ideal}} = 6.02N + 1.76\ \text{dB}. \]

A 14-bit converter could reach 86 dB. Real converters fall short because of thermal noise, clock jitter and nonlinearity; the effective number of bits is defined by inverting the formula with the measured SNR: \(\text{ENOB} = (\text{SNR} - 1.76)/6.02\). RF-sampling converters are usually specified by their noise spectral density instead (Chapter 22).

Processing gain: more bits by filtering

Quantisation and thermal noise spread over the whole Nyquist band \(f_s/2\). If you only care about a narrow bandwidth \(B\) (for example 1 MHz around your readout carrier), filtering away the rest removes most of the noise. The improvement is the processing gain

\[ G_p = 10\log_{10}\frac{f_s}{2B}. \]

At \(f_s = 4.42\) GSPS and \(B = 1\) MHz, \(G_p = 33\) dB. This is why integrating a readout signal in the FPGA gives much better SNR than the raw 14 bits suggest, and why the demodulate-and-integrate step (Chapter 23) is so powerful.

Clock jitter

If the sampling instants wobble by a random time \(\sigma_t\) (jitter), a signal of frequency \(f\) acquires a voltage error of order \(2\pi fA\sigma_t\). The resulting SNR limit is

\[ \text{SNR}_{\text{jitter}} = -20\log_{10}(2\pi f\sigma_t). \]

With \(\sigma_t = 100\) fs at 1 GHz, the limit is 64 dB; at 5 GHz it is 50 dB. High-frequency sampling needs very clean clocks, which is why the 4x2 has dedicated jitter-cleaning clock chips (Chapter 30).

Check yourself

1. A DAC at 9830.4 MSPS plays a 1200 MHz tone. Where are the first two images?

Show answer
At \(f_s - f = 8630.4\) MHz and \(f_s + f = 11030.4\) MHz, attenuated by the sinc response (and by the board's analog path).

2. What is the ideal SNR of a 12-bit converter? How much processing gain do you get filtering a 4 GSPS stream to 100 kHz?

Show answer
\(6.02\times12 + 1.76 = 74\) dB. \(G_p = 10\log_{10}(4\times10^9/2\times10^5) = 43\) dB.

3. Why does the even Nyquist zone appear mirrored?

Show answer
A frequency \(f\) in zone 2 aliases to \(f_s - f\); as \(f\) increases, \(f_s - f\) decreases, so the spectrum is reversed.
Summary
  • Sampling at \(f_s\) represents frequencies up to \(f_s/2\); higher frequencies alias into zone 1 at \(|f - kf_s|\).
  • DAC outputs roll off as sinc(\(f/f_s\)) and produce images at \(kf_s\pm f\); mix-mode favours zone 2.
  • On the 4x2 with QICK: DAC 9830.4 MSPS (zone 1 up to 4915 MHz), ADC 4423.68 MSPS (Nyquist 2212 MHz).
  • Ideal SNR is \(6.02N+1.76\) dB; filtering to a narrow bandwidth adds processing gain \(10\log(f_s/2B)\).
  • Clock jitter limits SNR at high frequency.
