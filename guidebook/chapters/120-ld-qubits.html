Why you need this

This is the qubit your device is built for: one electron spin per dot in Si/SiGe, rotated by microwaves applied to a gate, with a cobalt micromagnet converting electric motion into an effective magnetic drive. Understanding exactly how the micromagnet works tells you what microwave frequency and power you need, why the two qubits under one magnet have different frequencies, why charge noise dephases them, and what the RFSoC must generate. It also explains why your choice of magnetic field is really a choice of microwave hardware.

The Loss–DiVincenzo qubit

Loss and DiVincenzo proposed in 1998 that the spin of a single electron in each quantum dot could serve as a qubit, with single-qubit gates from local magnetic fields and two-qubit gates from pulsed exchange between neighbouring dots . Twenty-five years later this is the most widespread spin-qubit design: it is called the Loss–DiVincenzo (LD) qubit or single-spin qubit. Its operations map directly onto earlier chapters:

Driving with a real oscillating magnetic field (ESR)

The textbook approach is electron spin resonance: send a microwave current through an on-chip wire (a stripline or antenna) near the dot, producing an oscillating magnetic field \(B_1\). From Chapter 3, the Rabi frequency is \(f_R = g\mu_B B_1/(2h)\) for a linearly polarised field (only the co-rotating half drives the spin). In silicon this is 14 MHz per mT.

ESR was first demonstrated for a single electron in a GaAs double dot in 2006 , and on-chip ESR antennas are used in Si-MOS devices. The drawbacks are practical: producing even 1 mT at the dot needs milliamperes of microwave current, which heats the device, and the field also creates electric fields that shake the electrons. Rabi frequencies are typically below 1 MHz.

Driving with an electric field (EDSR)

Gates are much better at making electric fields than magnetic ones. Electric-dipole spin resonance applies a microwave voltage to a gate, which pushes the electron back and forth by a tiny distance. If the electron's spin is coupled to its position, that motion drives the spin. In GaAs and in germanium holes, intrinsic spin–orbit coupling provides this coupling (Chapter 10). In silicon electrons, spin–orbit coupling is weak, so a coupling is engineered with a magnetic field gradient .

The micromagnet mechanism

Place a ferromagnet (usually cobalt) on top of the device. Its stray field \(\vec B_{\text{mm}}(\vec r)\) varies with position. Now shake the electron along x with amplitude \(x_{ac}\):

\[ x(t) = x_{ac}\cos\omega t \quad\Rightarrow\quad \vec B(t) \approx \vec B_{\text{mm}}(0) + \frac{\partial\vec B_{\text{mm}}}{\partial x}\,x_{ac}\cos\omega t . \]

Split the gradient into two parts relative to the total static field direction (call it z):

  • the transverse gradient \(b_\perp = \partial B_\perp/\partial x\) produces an oscillating field perpendicular to z. This is exactly the drive term of Chapter 3, with amplitude \(B_1 = b_\perp x_{ac}\).
  • the longitudinal gradient \(b_\parallel = \partial B_z/\partial x\) makes the qubit frequency depend on position. It does not drive the spin, but it makes different dots have different frequencies, and it converts electron motion from charge noise into frequency noise.

The Rabi frequency is

\[ f_R = \frac{g\mu_B}{2h}\,b_\perp\,x_{ac} . \]

How large is \(x_{ac}\)? For an electron in a harmonic confining potential of orbital energy \(\hbar\omega_{\text{orb}}\), an AC electric field \(E_{ac}\) displaces it by \(x_{ac} = eE_{ac}/(m^*\omega_{\text{orb}}^2)\). Softer confinement (smaller \(\omega_{\text{orb}}\)) means larger displacement and faster Rabi oscillations, but also more sensitivity to charge noise.

Worked example

A micromagnet gives \(b_\perp = 0.5\) mT/nm at the dot. You want \(f_R = 5\) MHz. What displacement is needed?

\(f_R = 14\ \text{MHz/mT}\times b_\perp x_{ac}\), so \(b_\perp x_{ac} = 5/14 = 0.36\) mT and \(x_{ac} = 0.36/0.5 \approx 0.7\) nm. The electron moves by less than a nanometre, a few percent of the dot size (tens of nm). The microwave amplitude needed at the gate to do this is typically around a millivolt to a few millivolts.

The design trade-off

The micromagnet must provide three things at once:

  1. Large transverse gradient \(b_\perp\) at each dot, for fast Rabi oscillations.
  2. Different longitudinal fields at neighbouring dots, so that each qubit has its own frequency and a microwave burst at one frequency rotates only one qubit. Typical separations are tens to hundreds of MHz.
  3. Small longitudinal gradient \(b_\parallel\) at each dot, so that electron jitter from charge noise does not change the qubit frequency much.

Points 2 and 3 conflict: the field must change between dots but be flat at each dot. Yoneda and colleagues designed a magnet that kept \(b_\parallel\) small at the qubit location, reaching \(T_2^*\approx 20\ \mu\)s and over 99.9% single-qubit fidelity in purified silicon . The micromagnet must also be fully magnetised so its field is stable: cobalt micromagnets are usually operated with an in-plane external field of several hundred millitesla, which puts the qubit frequency around 10–20 GHz.

Spectroscopy and addressing several qubits

A device with two qubits under one micromagnet shows two EDSR lines. Their frequencies are \(f_{1,2} = g\mu_B(B_0 + B_{\text{mm},1,2})/h\). Each qubit is addressed by driving at its own frequency, often through the same gate. Several practical issues appear:

  • Crosstalk: a strong drive at \(f_1\) slightly rotates qubit 2 if \(f_2 - f_1\) is not large compared with the Rabi frequency. Smooth pulse envelopes (Chapter 3) and frequency separation reduce this.
  • AC Stark shifts: driving qubit 1 shifts qubit 2's frequency, leading to phase errors on qubit 2. They are calibrated and compensated with virtual Z rotations .
  • Microwave-induced heating: in several silicon devices, applying microwaves shifts qubit frequencies by tens of kHz to MHz, apparently through heating. Groups keep the total microwave power constant over time, for example with off-resonant "dummy" pulses .

Other single-spin drive methods

  • Intrinsic spin–orbit EDSR in GaAs and in germanium holes, with no micromagnet. Hole qubits in germanium reach Rabi frequencies of tens of MHz this way .
  • g-tensor modulation: a gate voltage changes the g-factor's direction or size, which drives the spin. Important for holes and some Si-MOS devices.
  • Flopping-mode EDSR: drive an electron between two dots near the interdot transition, so that it moves a large distance through the gradient. This gives fast Rabi frequencies at low microwave power, at the cost of more charge-noise sensitivity .
In the lab

The microwave reaches the device through a dedicated attenuated coaxial line, often combined with the DC voltage of a gate in a bias tee on the sample PCB. Typical source powers are −10 to +10 dBm at room temperature with 20–40 dB of attenuation in the fridge, so the gate sees roughly −30 to −50 dBm (about 1–10 mV amplitude across 50 Ω). Groups tune the power by watching the Rabi frequency, which should rise linearly with drive amplitude until heating or nonlinear effects appear.

On the 4x2

EDSR is where the frequency plan meets your magnet:

  • Direct synthesis, first Nyquist zone (up to about 4.9 GHz): needs the total field at the dot to be below about 0.175 T. A cobalt micromagnet may not be fully magnetised at such low external fields, and readout by Elzerman would be thermally limited. Possible, but not the usual operating point.
  • Second Nyquist zone (about 4.9–9.8 GHz): QICK lets a generator use the DAC's second Nyquist zone (nqz=2). Output power falls with frequency because of the DAC's sinc roll-off and analog bandwidth, so check the actual power with a spectrum analyser. This reaches fields of roughly 0.18–0.35 T.
  • External upconversion (any frequency): generate an intermediate frequency (IF) of a few hundred MHz to ~1–2 GHz on one DAC, mix it with a local oscillator (LO) from a separate microwave source, and filter out the unwanted sideband. With two DACs you can feed the I and Q inputs of an IQ mixer for clean single-sideband output, but that uses both outputs and leaves none for readout. Chapter 20 explains the trade-offs, and Chapter 42 gives a full EDSR setup.

Because the 4x2's DACs are AC-coupled, they are fine for microwave bursts, which have no DC component. Microwave drive is a natural job for the board.

Check yourself

1. Two qubits sit 100 nm apart under a micromagnet whose longitudinal field changes by 0.2 mT/nm between them. What is their frequency difference?

Show answer
\(\Delta B_z = 0.2\times100 = 20\) mT, so \(\Delta f = 28\ \text{GHz/T}\times0.02\ \text{T} = 560\) MHz (if the gradient were uniform; real field profiles are not, but the order of magnitude is typical).

2. Why does a larger longitudinal gradient at the dot reduce \(T_2^*\)?

Show answer
Charge noise moves the electron slightly. With a longitudinal gradient, position fluctuations become qubit-frequency fluctuations, \(\delta f = (g\mu_B/h)\,b_\parallel\,\delta x\), which dephase the qubit.

3. Your qubit is at 13.2 GHz. Can the 4x2 drive it directly?

Show answer
Not directly in the first or second Nyquist zone at 9.83 GSPS (above about 9.8 GHz is the third zone, with very low power and above the balun rating). Use an external LO and mixer: for example, an LO at 12 GHz and a 1.2 GHz IF from one DAC, with a filter to remove the 10.8 GHz image.
Summary
  • LD qubits use one electron spin per dot, ESR/EDSR for single-qubit gates, exchange for two-qubit gates, and spin-to-charge readout.
  • ESR uses a real oscillating magnetic field; EDSR shakes the electron electrically and needs spin–orbit coupling or a field gradient.
  • With a micromagnet, \(f_R = (g\mu_B/2h)\,b_\perp x_{ac}\); the longitudinal field difference separates qubit frequencies; the longitudinal gradient at each dot adds charge-noise dephasing.
  • Co micromagnets are usually operated at several hundred mT, putting qubit frequencies at 10–20 GHz, which requires external upconversion with the 4x2.
