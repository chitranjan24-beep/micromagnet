Why you need this

"I and Q" appear everywhere in qubit control: the readout returns I/Q values, microwave pulses are built from I/Q envelopes, and IQ mixers shift waveforms to GHz. The reason is that a sinusoid of a given frequency has two independent knobs, amplitude and phase, and I/Q is the cleanest way to handle both. This chapter builds the idea from complex numbers, then explains mixers, numerically controlled oscillators (NCOs) and the digital up- and down-converters inside the RFSoC and QICK. It follows the SDR book's chapters on quadrature modulation and SDR architectures .

A sinusoid as a rotating arrow

Euler's formula \(e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta + i\sin\theta\) lets us write any sinusoid as the real part of a rotating complex number:

\[ A\cos(\omega t + \phi) = \Re\big\{A e^{i\phi}\,e^{i\omega t}\big\}. \]

The complex number \(\tilde A = Ae^{i\phi}\) is the phasor. It contains everything about the signal except its frequency. If the amplitude and phase change slowly over time, \(\tilde A(t)\) is called the complex envelope.

Write the phasor in Cartesian form, \(\tilde A = I + iQ\), with \(I = A\cos\phi\) and \(Q = A\sin\phi\). Expanding the cosine:

\[ A\cos(\omega t+\phi) = I\cos\omega t - Q\sin\omega t . \]

So any sinusoid at frequency \(\omega\) is a sum of a cosine with weight \(I\) (the in-phase component) and a sine with weight \(Q\) (the quadrature component, 90° shifted). Amplitude is \(\sqrt{I^2+Q^2}\), phase is \(\operatorname{atan2}(Q, I)\).

Mixing: multiplying signals shifts frequencies

A mixer multiplies two signals. From \(\cos a\cos b = \tfrac12[\cos(a-b) + \cos(a+b)]\), multiplying a signal at frequency \(f_{IF}\) (the intermediate frequency) by a local oscillator at \(f_{LO}\) produces two outputs, at \(f_{LO} - f_{IF}\) and \(f_{LO}+f_{IF}\). These are the lower and upper sidebands. Usually you want only one; the other is the image.

Single-sideband (IQ) upconversion

An IQ mixer contains two mixers driven by copies of the LO 90° apart. Feed it baseband I and Q signals at the IF:

\[ I(t) = \cos\omega_{IF}t,\quad Q(t) = \sin\omega_{IF}t \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; I\cos\omega_{LO}t - Q\sin\omega_{LO}t = \cos\big((\omega_{LO}+\omega_{IF})t\big). \]

The image cancels exactly, leaving only the upper sideband. Swapping the sign of Q selects the lower sideband. Real mixers are imperfect: unequal gains and a phase error of a degree or so leave an image at −25 to −40 dBc, and a small DC offset on I or Q lets the LO leak through. Both are calibrated by adjusting small offsets and amplitude/phase corrections while watching a spectrum analyser. IQ upconversion is how commercial controllers produce qubit drive at 10–20 GHz .

Downconversion and demodulation

The reverse operation recovers I and Q from a received signal \(r(t) = A\cos(\omega t + \phi)\). Multiply it by \(\cos\omega t\) and by \(-\sin\omega t\):

\[ r(t)\cos\omega t = \tfrac{A}{2}\cos\phi + \tfrac{A}{2}\cos(2\omega t + \phi), \qquad -r(t)\sin\omega t = \tfrac{A}{2}\sin\phi - \tfrac{A}{2}\sin(2\omega t+\phi). \]

A low-pass filter removes the \(2\omega\) terms, leaving \(I/2\) and \(Q/2\). In complex notation this is a single multiplication by \(e^{-i\omega t}\) followed by filtering. This is the core of every reflectometry receiver, lock-in amplifier and qubit readout chain.

Numerically controlled oscillators

In digital hardware, the reference sinusoids are generated by a numerically controlled oscillator (NCO), also called direct digital synthesis (DDS). Each clock cycle, an \(N\)-bit phase accumulator adds a frequency word \(W\):

\[ \theta[n+1] = \theta[n] + W \pmod{2^N}, \qquad f = \frac{W}{2^N}f_{\text{clk}} . \]

The accumulator value is converted to \(\cos\theta\) and \(\sin\theta\) (by a lookup table or a CORDIC circuit). The frequency resolution is \(f_{\text{clk}}/2^N\), and the phase stays continuous when \(W\) changes, which is exactly what qubit control needs.

Worked example: QICK frequency resolution

QICK's generators use 32-bit DDS running at the DAC sample rate. On the 4x2, \(9830.4\ \text{MHz}/2^{32} = 2.29\) Hz. The QICK documentation quotes 2.3 Hz for the generator and 20.6 Hz for the common step that both a generator and a readout can hit exactly . A 9 Hz mismatch between them would rotate the demodulated phase once every 0.1 s, which is why QICK provides frequency matching (Chapter 36).

Digital up- and down-conversion in the RFSoC

The RFSoC's data converters contain hardened digital up-converters (DUC) and digital down-converters (DDC): interpolation or decimation filters, a complex mixer with an NCO (a 48-bit fine mixer, plus a coarse mixer for fixed shifts like \(f_s/4\)), and quadrature-error correction. In Gen3 devices, interpolation and decimation factors from 1× to 40× are available . A 5G radio uses them to move low-rate baseband data to GHz without burdening the FPGA.

QICK takes a different approach on the 4x2. Its standard firmware sets the DACs to 1× interpolation and the ADCs to 1× decimation, and does the frequency synthesis and down-conversion in the FPGA fabric itself :

  • Each signal generator computes 16 output samples per fabric clock cycle (614.4 MHz × 16 = 9830.4 MSPS), multiplying its envelope by its own DDS, so the output frequency, phase and amplitude can change from one pulse to the next under tProcessor control.
  • Each readout takes the full-rate ADC stream, mixes it with its own DDS, filters and decimates by 8 (to 552.96 MSPS on the 4x2), and accumulates over the readout window.

Doing it in fabric gives QICK precise, per-pulse control and phase coherence between generators and readouts, at the cost of FPGA resources. Chapter 33 describes these blocks in detail.

Common mistake

Forgetting that the external LO is a separate oscillator. Its phase is not locked to the RFSoC unless both share a 10 MHz reference. Without a shared reference, the qubit drive phase drifts relative to QICK's timing, and Ramsey or echo experiments with long sequences lose phase coherence. Lock the LO and the 4x2 (via its external reference input, Chapter 30) to the same 10 MHz clock.

Check yourself

1. A signal has I = 0.3, Q = −0.4. What are its amplitude and phase?

Show answer
Amplitude \(\sqrt{0.09+0.16} = 0.5\); phase \(\operatorname{atan2}(-0.4, 0.3) \approx -53°\).

2. With an LO at 14 GHz and an IF of 600 MHz into a simple mixer, where are the outputs? How far apart are wanted and image?

Show answer
13.4 and 14.6 GHz, 1.2 GHz apart (\(2f_{IF}\)). Filtering one out needs a sharp filter; an IF of 1.5 GHz would separate them by 3 GHz.

3. Why must the phase accumulator be continuous when the frequency word changes?

Show answer
So that the oscillator's phase, which is the qubit's reference frame, does not jump. A phase jump would rotate the effective drive axis and corrupt subsequent gates.
Summary
  • A sinusoid at known frequency is a phasor \(I + iQ\): amplitude and phase.
  • Mixers multiply signals, producing sum and difference frequencies; IQ mixers cancel the image for single-sideband output.
  • Demodulation multiplies by \(e^{-i\omega t}\) and low-pass filters, giving I and Q.
  • NCOs (DDS) generate phase-continuous sinusoids with resolution \(f_{\text{clk}}/2^N\); QICK uses 32-bit DDS (2.3 Hz steps on the 4x2).
  • QICK synthesises and demodulates in FPGA fabric; external mixers and an LO extend the 4x2's range for EDSR.
