Why you need this

This chapter connects everything in Part V to the readout of your qubit. The ADC delivers billions of samples per second; you want, for every shot, one bit: spin up or down. In between are down-conversion, filtering, choosing the right time window, integrating, rotating the IQ plane and thresholding. On the RFSoC, almost all of this happens in FPGA logic in real time, which is why the RFSoC can make decisions (feedback) within a microsecond of a measurement. Understanding each step lets you set QICK's readout parameters sensibly and squeeze the most fidelity out of your sensor.

The readout pipeline

Step 1: down-conversion

The readout's DDS generates \(e^{-i\omega t}\) at the carrier frequency. Multiplying turns the reflected carrier into a slowly varying complex number, \(s(t) = I(t) + iQ(t)\), proportional to the reflection coefficient \(\Gamma\) of the tank circuit, plus noise. Because the generator that produced the carrier and the readout's DDS run from the same clock and (with frequency matching) the same frequency, \(s(t)\) is constant in time for a constant \(\Gamma\), shot after shot .

Step 2: filtering and decimation

A low-pass filter removes the \(2\omega\) component and noise outside the band; then the stream is decimated. On the 4x2, QICK's readout decimates by 8, from 4423.68 to 552.96 MSPS . These decimated samples can be recorded directly: QICK's acquire_decimated() returns the averaged I/Q trace versus time, which is how you see the carrier arrive, check the timing, and look at the sensor's response during a pulse.

Step 3: choosing the readout window

The signal does not appear at the ADC at the moment the DAC starts the carrier. It takes time to travel: cables down and up a fridge (several metres, about 5 ns/m), amplifiers, filters, and the converters' own pipelines. This delay is the time of flight, typically 0.2–1 µs in total. The tank resonator also takes time to ring up: its response time is about \(Q/(\pi f_0)\), where \(Q\) is its quality factor. For \(Q = 30\) at 200 MHz, that is about 48 ns.

So: measure the time of flight once with a decimated trace (Chapter 36), then start the readout window after the signal has arrived and settled. In QICK this is the trigger time of the readout relative to the pulse, plus a calibrated offset.

Step 4: integration

Summing the decimated samples over a window of length \(T\) gives one complex number per shot. For white noise, the signal grows as \(T\) and the noise as \(\sqrt T\), so SNR grows as \(\sqrt T\) until something else (relaxation, drifts) intervenes. QICK accumulates the samples in hardware and returns either every shot's sum or the average over shots.

Weighted integration

If the signal changes during the window, for example because a triplet relaxes to a singlet during PSB readout, equal weights are not optimal. The SNR-optimal linear filter for distinguishing two known average traces \(\bar s_0(t)\) and \(\bar s_1(t)\) in white noise is the matched filter, with weights \(w(t) \propto \bar s_1(t) - \bar s_0(t)\). If the difference decays because of relaxation, the weights decay too, trusting early samples more.

Step 5: the IQ plane, rotation and threshold

Plot each shot's integrated \((I, Q)\) as a point. Shots from the two states form two clouds. The direction joining the cloud centres carries the information; the perpendicular direction only adds noise. Rotate the plane by the angle \(\theta = \operatorname{atan2}(\Delta Q, \Delta I)\) of the separation vector, so that all the signal is on the new \(I'\) axis, and discard \(Q'\). Then set a threshold on \(I'\) between the clouds.

Worked example: from traces to SNR

Your averaged decimated traces for the two charge states differ by 40 ADC units in \(I'\) during a 2 µs window; single decimated samples have a noise standard deviation of 400 units. At 552.96 MSPS, 2 µs contains about 1106 samples. Summing them (and dividing by the count) reduces the noise by \(\sqrt{1106} \approx 33\), to about 12 units. The single-shot SNR is \(40/12 \approx 3.3\), giving about 5% error per state (Chapter 5). To reach SNR 6 you would need about \((6/3.3)^2 \approx 3.3\) times longer: 6.6 µs, if relaxation allows.

Averaged versus single-shot data

  • Averaged: average I/Q over many shots, then rotate and scale to a probability using calibration points (prepared 0 and 1). Robust and simple, but SPAM and readout errors blend into the result.
  • Single-shot: threshold each shot, then count. Gives true probabilities (after correcting for readout errors) and correlations between qubits. Required for feedback, error correction and many benchmarking protocols.

The same pipeline as a lock-in

If you replace the GHz carrier with a lower-frequency excitation (HRL used 1 MHz through the charge sensor, read with a 20 kΩ current-to-voltage resistor ), the same mix–filter–integrate chain is a digital lock-in amplifier. On the 4x2 such a low frequency is below the baluns' 10 MHz rating, so expect strong attenuation unless you bypass them; reflectometry at hundreds of MHz avoids the problem.

On the 4x2

The 4x2's QICK readouts provide, per channel, an accumulated buffer of 16 384 entries (one per shot or per sweep point) and a decimated buffer of 4096 samples (7.4 µs at 552.96 MSPS). A 1.07-billion-sample DDR4 buffer (1.94 s) can record long continuous traces, useful for watching tunnelling events, and a multi-rate buffer captures raw 8192-sample stretches . These numbers come from the firmware's configuration printout; print soc to see your own firmware's values.

What the 4x2 can demodulate

Demodulation on the 4x2 is just arithmetic in the FPGA, so the board can demodulate any signal that reaches its ADC cleanly. What decides that is the analog path in front of the ADC. Each ADC input passes through two 100 nF capacitors and a balun rated for 10 MHz to 10 GHz (Chapter 29) . The ADC then samples at 4423.68 MSPS in the standard QICK firmware, so the first Nyquist zone ends at 2211.84 MHz .

Why there is a lower limit. A balun is a small transformer. At low frequency its winding inductance has a low impedance and shorts the signal, so the transformer stops coupling energy across. The series capacitors add a second high-pass corner and block DC entirely. Together they set the 10 MHz floor. This is the same reason the board cannot output DC gate pulses (Chapter 29).

What to do with low-frequency signals:

  • Keep a lock-in amplifier for DC and kHz transport during the first cooldowns (Coulomb diamonds, pinch-offs). Most groups do this anyway.
  • Move to RF reflectometry. It gives the same charge information at a carrier of hundreds of MHz and is much faster (Chapter 9).
  • Modify the ADC input to make it DC-coupled, as the QICK-DAWG team did with an LMH5401 amplifier . Then the same demodulation chain works as a digital lock-in at kHz–MHz.

Other limits to know.

  • Number of tones. The standard firmware has two readout channels, each on its own ADC input, and each demodulates one frequency at a time. The frequency can change between shots. Reading several sensors on one line by frequency multiplexing needs multiplexed firmware or a second readout channel (Chapters 9 and 33).
  • Signal level. The reflected signal from a tank circuit is tiny. It needs cryogenic and room-temperature amplification to sit well above the ADC's noise floor (Chapter 22).
  • Bandwidth is not a limit. After decimation the demodulated signal has 552.96 MSPS, so it can follow changes on nanosecond scales. The tank circuit, with a bandwidth of a few MHz, is always the slower part.
Check yourself

1. Your two IQ clouds are centred at (100, 20) and (40, 100). By what angle should you rotate so that the separation lies along +I?

Show answer
The separation vector is (40 − 100, 100 − 20) = (−60, 80), at angle atan2(80, −60) ≈ 126.9°. Rotating by −126.9° puts it along +I (or by 53.1° to put it along −I; either works if the threshold is placed accordingly).

2. Why does integration time stop improving fidelity beyond a certain point?

Show answer
During longer windows more excited states relax (or the charge state changes for other reasons), so the classes overlap more. Slow drifts and 1/f noise also stop averaging down as \(\sqrt T\).

3. Your lab measures the sensor dot with a 77 Hz lock-in excitation. Can you connect that signal to a 4x2 ADC input and demodulate it there?

Show answer
No. 77 Hz is far below the 10 MHz lower limit of the ADC input's balun and capacitors, so the signal does not reach the converter. Keep the lock-in for this measurement, or measure the same sensor with RF reflectometry at hundreds of MHz, which the 4x2 handles directly.

4. A colleague's tank circuit resonates at 3.0 GHz. Can the 4x2 demodulate it, and at what frequency does the signal appear after sampling at 4423.68 MSPS?

Show answer
Yes, by RF sampling in the second Nyquist zone (2211.84–4423.68 MHz) with a band-pass filter around 3 GHz. The signal folds to 4423.68 − 3000 = 1423.68 MHz. In the second zone the spectrum is reversed, so the IQ plane is mirrored (Q changes sign) and phase shifts appear with the opposite sign. Thresholding still works; just account for the sign when you interpret phases. The DAC can generate 3.0 GHz directly because it is inside the DAC's first zone (up to 4915.2 MHz).
Summary
  • Readout: mix with the carrier, filter and decimate, integrate over a window, rotate, threshold.
  • Start the window after the time of flight and resonator ring-up.
  • SNR grows as \(\sqrt T\) for white noise; matched (weighted) filters do better when the signal decays.
  • Single-shot thresholding gives bits and enables feedback; averaging gives probabilities with calibration.
  • QICK does mixing, decimation and accumulation in the FPGA, with buffers for shots, traces and long records.
  • The 4x2 demodulates any carrier from about 10 MHz to 2.2 GHz directly, and higher ones with band-pass filtering. kHz lock-in signals and DC are blocked by the AC-coupled input unless the input is modified.
